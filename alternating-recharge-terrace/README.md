# Alternating Recharge Terrace

**Author: jayis1. Status: research concept (TRL 2), not a drinking-water treatment device.**

## Problem

Groundwater supplies roughly half the water withdrawn for domestic use worldwide, according to UNESCO.[1] In water-scarce catchments, brief storm flows can leave before they replenish a usable shallow aquifer. Managed aquifer recharge (MAR) already diverts water into infiltration basins, but suspended silt and biological films clog the basin surface and require drying and scraping.[2] An unmaintained basin can lose capacity; diverting polluted stormwater can also make a drinking-water aquifer less safe. The intended users are communities and smallholder water cooperatives, with permission from downstream water users and the watershed authority—not individual households pumping an untested aquifer.

## Solution

Build **two shallow, parallel 25 m² infiltration terraces**, each with a replaceable top layer of washed local sand over surveyed native permeable soil. A 1 m³ masonry or lined-earth settling forebay intercepts first-flush sediment. A **manual three-position sluice** sends eligible settled water to one terrace at a time or returns it to the original channel; the resting terrace drains and dries so a local crew can remove its thin silt crust and replace washed sand without excavating the infiltration subsoil. A weir and armored overflow preserve the stream's original high-flow route. No injection bore, chemicals, pump, sensor network, or private extraction entitlement is implied.

A trained operator opens a terrace only after the site's approved source-water and flow checks; first flush, suspected sewage or pesticide contamination, insufficient downstream flow, and uncertain water quality mean **bypass**, not recharge. The system does not purify water to drinking standards. Well setbacks, water rights, flood hydraulics, groundwater mounding and aquifer suitability require a hydrogeologist and local authority before construction. A locally readable staff gauge and a timed bucket-and-level test record inflow, standing depth and infiltration decline; they are operational observations, not proof of groundwater storage.

## Key Innovation

Surface MAR, sediment forebays, wet/dry cycling and sand scraping are all established practice.[2][3] This proposal's testable advance is a **small, repairable dual-bed exchange geometry** in which the dirty upper sand skin can be lifted from a resting terrace while the other terrace accepts an approved flow; the common three-position sluice has a positive *bypass* state, so maintenance need not silently turn into continued infiltration through a clogged bed. The research question is whether this service interface preserves usable infiltration capacity and makes maintenance less disruptive than a single basin of comparable area—not whether it invents recharge itself. It differs from this repository's [Paddy Water-Table Gate](../paddy-water-table-gate/), which controls irrigation in rice plots, and [Flood-Isolating Wellhead Collar](../flood-isolating-wellhead-collar/), which tries to exclude floodwater from an existing well. Here the objective is controlled *replenishment of a surveyed aquifer* without claiming potable-water treatment.

**Unverified prototype targets:** active-bed wetted area 25 m²; measured sustained infiltration flux at least 0.03 m/h for a six-hour eligible event; 0.25 L/s maximum inlet; ≤0.15 m operating ponding depth; and 15 qualified events/year only as a planning scenario, not a climate forecast. The bed must be taken offline when a site-specific decline threshold is reached; it is never forced with extra pressure. Actual flux depends on native soil, antecedent moisture, water chemistry, water-table elevation and clogging, and could be near zero.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 goal at 1,000 installations/year:** US$1,300 installed per two-bed site: earthworks and local sand US$470, stone/masonry forebay and overflow US$310, HDPE/steel sluice and replaceable liners US$230, labor and commissioning US$290. Land acquisition, permits, hydrogeological survey, source-water laboratory tests, downstream-flow mitigation, finance and taxes are excluded and may dominate hardware cost. Budget a further **US$100–200/site-year** for inspections, sand replacement, sediment disposal and water tests; confirm locally rather than assuming volunteer labor. Community/cooperative or watershed authority pays, with public funding where benefits are shared. Publish gate dimensions and permit local fabrication and repair.

## Impact

The design calculation is a ceiling, **not measured recharge**: `25 m² × 0.03 m/h × 6 h = 4.5 m³` entering the active bed per qualifying event. A 0.25 L/s inlet can deliver at most 5.4 m³ in six hours, so it does not contradict that target; the 1 m³ forebay has roughly 1.1 h nominal residence at that flow, but that alone does not guarantee sediment or pathogen removal. Even if 15 such events occur, the upper-bound infiltrated volume is **67.5 m³/site-year** before evapotranspiration, vadose-zone losses and aquifer recovery losses. Ten thousand eligible sites would have a *planning ceiling* of 675,000 m³/year infiltrated, not 675,000 m³ of safe drinking water or net new supply. Recharge can merely move water between users; no benefit is assigned without downstream-flow and paired-well evidence.

A 10–20-year development path uses existing earthworks and water-testing practice: pilot 20 hydrogeologically approved sites against 20 single-bed basins of equal total footprint, measure event-by-event inlet/bypass volumes, infiltration decline, cleaning labor, adjacent observation-well level and chemistry, and downstream flows for two wet seasons. Pre-register an incremental target of **≥20% more cumulative qualified infiltration per square metre of total bed footprint** than the single-bed comparator, with **no deterioration in groundwater quality or downstream allocated flow**. This is unproven, and a cheaper single basin or demand reduction may perform better. No health outcomes, aquifer recovery or avoided depletion are claimed from a basin inflow number alone.

## Safety, failure modes and validation

- **Contamination:** never connect to a drinking-water well or bypass the unsaturated zone. Baseline and follow-up samples must include locally relevant microbial indicators, nitrate, salinity and industrial/agricultural contaminants; if a source fails its permit limits or monitoring is unavailable, keep the gate in bypass and do not use the terrace. Sand is not a reliable disinfection barrier.
- **Clogging or gate neglect:** a resting bed is dried, lifted and cleaned by a trained crew; bag and dispose of contaminated scrapings under local rules. If both beds clog or the sluice jams, close the inlet and pass flow through the protected original route. No automated claim of self-cleaning.
- **Flood, erosion and ecological harm:** engineer the bypass for the locally approved design flood, test embankment stability and overflow scour, retain environmental flows and public access, and do not site in a floodplain without approval. A blocked overflow requires closure and repair, not raised ponding.
- **Groundwater harm:** require infiltration tests, depth-to-water and mounding assessment, compatible geochemistry, setback from latrines/contaminated land and permits. Rising contaminated water, salinity, shallow mounding or downstream-flow deficits stop recharge pending investigation. Wells remain subject to independent potable-water testing.

## How It Works

The novelty is not wet/dry MAR cycling—conventional infiltration basins already do that [2][3]—but a two-bed, replaceable surface coupled to a deliberate bypass state that lets a small crew service one bed while the other remains available. Survey native soil, groundwater depth and embankment stability first; neither a sand layer nor a terrace creates permeability where the subsoil is impermeable. During an approved storm, send first flush down the original channel, settle an eligible later fraction in the forebay, then direct no more than 0.25 L/s to bed A. Water ponds no deeper than 0.15 m and passes through sand and native unsaturated soil under gravity. Log inlet volume, water level and drawdown; infiltration at the surface is not proof that the same volume reaches a usable aquifer. When drawdown deteriorates, switch to bed B only after the same checks, let A drain and dry, then remove and replace its clogged upper sand skin. If no approved bed or operator is available, bypass. The original stream route remains available during a flood.

## Technical Architecture

`source/first-flush bypass → 1 m³ forebay → manual three-position sluice → bed A or B (25 m² each) → native vadose zone → surveyed aquifer`; the bypass and armored overflow return excess water to the original channel. A staff gauge, timed inflow measurement and maintenance log provide local operational data; observation wells and laboratory samples belong to a separately permitted monitoring plan. The sluice is manual, not remotely actuated: an operator verifies source eligibility, downstream allocation and bed condition at every event. A jammed gate, full forebay, blocked overflow or missing water-quality result means stop diversion and restore the protected bypass, subject to the site engineer's flood-safe procedure. The hydraulic design must not depend on the operator holding back a design flood.

## Performance Benchmarks

No terrace prototype has been measured. Conventional wet/dry infiltration basins, sediment control and scraping are the **current practice**, not an inferior numerical benchmark [2][3]. Comparable published rate and cost figures are not established for this specific soil, water source and 50 m² footprint; measure a same-footprint single-bed control rather than borrowing an unrelated MAR headline number.

| Metric and conditions | Existing practice / evidence | Proposed target (unverified) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sustained surface infiltration, eligible six-hour event | Site-dependent; establish with paired field tests | ≥0.03 m/h on the active 25 m² bed, capped by inlet supply |
| Qualified infiltrated volume per event | Measure single-bed comparator under matched inflow and soil | Up to 4.5 m³ at target flux, subject to 5.4 m³ inlet ceiling |
| Cumulative qualified infiltration per 50 m² total bed footprint, two wet seasons | Matched single-bed basin; no measured result yet | ≥20% above control, without water-quality or downstream-flow deterioration |
| Operating depth and maintenance | Site-specific engineered limits and measured crew hours | ≤0.15 m ponding; log labor, sand disposal and bypass time |

A six-hour `25 × 0.03 × 6` calculation assumes sustained flux and sufficient eligible water; it is not a guaranteed event yield. Compare sampled source and observation-well chemistry, mounding, environmental flow and actual labor alongside volume. ## Deployment Scenarios

- **Seasonal smallholder watershed:** A cooperative with suitable permeable terrace subsoil and lawful late-storm diversion operates one two-bed site beside an existing drainage route. Without a trained operator and approved source testing, the device stays in bypass; it does not license additional pumping.
- **Watershed trust pilot:** A district installs 20 approved terraces and 20 equal-footprint single-bed controls across matched subcatchments. Over two wet seasons it measures event flux, cleaning hours, observation-well response, chemistry and downstream flow before any claim of incremental recharge.
- **Peri-urban runoff edge:** A utility may test a site outside contaminated catchments with monitored stormwater and adequate well setbacks. Known sewage, industrial runoff or pesticide pulses are excluded, not treated by the sand bed.

## Risks & Mitigations

The existing safety rules above are operating requirements, not proof of safety. Fine sediment and biofilm can clog **both** beds: measure declining drawdown, clean the resting surface and bypass if capacity is inadequate. Sand can pass contaminants, while recharge may mobilize native metals or salts: screen sources and geochemistry, sample monitoring wells and stop on permit exceedances; potable wells need independent testing. On slopes, piping, shallow groundwater mounding and overflow scour can damage neighbors: require geotechnical/hydrogeological design, freeboard, a protected flood path and site-specific stop levels. Standing water can breed mosquitoes: inspect and drain on the approved operating schedule; never hold water to meet a volume target. Maintenance, disposal, lab fees and land rights can outweigh a low hardware quote: budget paid labor, obtain permits and protect downstream allocations. Residual risk and unknown aquifer recovery remain even if the inlet meter shows good infiltration.

## Vision for 2050

If paired trials demonstrate safe net recharge, affordable maintenance and protected downstream flows, local watershed groups could deploy repairable dual-bed terraces at selected sites instead of losing every suitable storm pulse. A network of 10,000 sites operating at the stated 15-event planning assumption would have an upper bound of 675,000 m³/year **infiltrating at the surface**, not a promise of aquifer storage or drinking water. By 2050 the valuable infrastructure would be the verified water accounting, trained operators and enforceable bypass decisions as much as the earthworks. If those checks fail, the right future is fewer terraces, not wider deployment.
