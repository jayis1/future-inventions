# Altitude-Qualified Lake Drain

**Author: jayis1** · Concept (TRL 2); not an approved glacial-lake intervention

## Problem

A global exposure study estimates that 15 million people live near potential glacial-lake outburst flood runout paths; exposure does not mean that any particular lake will burst.[1] Lowering a dangerous lake may reduce some modeled flood intensities, but an unsuitable drain can erode a moraine or fail silently. In Bhutan, a constructed 2025 siphon pilot at Thorthormi Lake never sustained flow; its surveyed crest lift was too high for the low atmospheric pressure at roughly 4,300 m, among other site and valve problems.[2]

## Solution

A **site-qualified, removable, gravity-driven lake-level reduction module** for small, professionally assessed moraine-dammed lakes with a safe existing downstream channel. Before installation, an engineer surveys the crest, lake level, receiving channel, lake hypsometry and dam stability. A portable barometer, water-vapor/temperature allowance and elevation profile provide a *no-go* check on available siphon suction head. A guarded HDPE intake sits below wave action but above sediment; pressure-rated HDPE pipe crosses a stable route without cutting the dam. A priming port, pressure taps at the apex, manual isolation valves and a restrained outlet into an engineered energy-dissipation basin permit controlled operation and observation. A staff gauge at the lake and a calibrated downstream flow measurement give an independent daily water balance; a loss of prime, unexpected level change, new seepage or outlet scour means stop, isolate and investigate. The system does **not** self-start or guarantee flood protection.

## Key Innovation

Siphons, lake lowering and pressure measurements are prior art.[2] The proposed integration is an **altitude-aware hydraulic eligibility gate coupled to an independently observed water-balance and outlet-erosion gate**: a portable package explicitly refuses sites where a siphon cannot work, and treats flow without safe downstream conveyance as failure rather than success. Unlike [Slope-Head Sentinel](../slope-head-sentinel/), which indicates local shallow-landslide groundwater head, this physically removes a bounded volume from an assessed lake. It is not an early-warning system, a substitute for an engineered spillway, or appropriate for ice-dammed lakes, unstable outlet slopes or sites without safe access.

A bench prototype target is a 100 mm internal-diameter HDPE line of at most 100 m, a surveyed **net surface-to-outlet drop of 5 m**, a crest no more than **3 m above the lowest operating lake surface** at a site with measured pressure at least **60 kPa**. Atmospheric pressure alone at 60 kPa supports only about 6.1 m of ideal water column (P/ρg at ρ ≈ 1,000 kg/m³); vapor pressure, dynamic losses and safety margin shrink that substantially. The 3 m figure is a conservative **design-screen target, not a universal operating limit**. A qualified hydraulic design must calculate every crest pressure, transient, friction and cavitation margin for the specific elevation, pipe and temperature. If the profile fails, do not install a siphon; consider an engineered alternative.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 hardware target at 1,000 kits/year:** US$3,000 per 100 m module: US$1,200 pressure-rated HDPE pipe and couplers; US$400 guarded intake and anchors; US$550 valves, priming and pressure fittings; US$350 outlet diffuser and restraint hardware; US$500 assembly, bench leak test and spares. Installation, survey, bathymetry, geotechnical and ecological studies, helicopter/porter logistics, permits, downstream works, maintenance and emergency preparedness are excluded and may dominate total project cost. Budget trained daily checks while operating, post-storm checks, replacement seals and safe retrieval of all plastic and metal. Compare full installed cost with a professionally designed channel or no-intervention alternative; no cost advantage is established.

## Impact

**Unverified engineering challenge:** at 5 L/s measured continuous flow, one module would move 432 m³/day (0.005 m³/s × 86,400 s/day). Across an assumed 1 ha near-surface lake area, that equals only 4.32 cm/day *before* inflow, precipitation, evaporation and changing area; a nominal 0.5 m lowering would require at least about 12 days under that simplified constant-area/no-inflow assumption. Larger lakes or high inflows can make the design ineffective. A ten-site, regulator-supervised pilot could measure achieved net drawdown, outlet scour, seepage and reliability against surveyed controls; it would **not** establish deaths prevented. Any eventual reach to the exposed population depends on site selection, modeled flood pathways, permissions and maintained downstream warnings.[1]

### Limits and validation

- Laboratory-test flow and negative apex pressure at the lowest intended lake level, altitude-equivalent pressure, temperature extremes, deliberate air leaks and blocked intakes. Proposed pass gate: ≥5 L/s in the specified 5 m-drop test for 72 h, with no unplanned loss of prime; an independent meter checks the water balance. Failure disproves the central operating target.
- A civil/geotechnical authority must model avalanche-wave overtopping, moraine piping, bank erosion and downstream flood routing before any field drawdown. A pressure-feasible site can still be unsafe. Cap discharge to a site-specific permitted rate, not automatically to 5 L/s; instrument seepage and scour and define stop thresholds *before* starting. Use warning systems and evacuation planning regardless.
- Ice, debris, UV cracking, air ingress, freeze-thaw and moving lake shores can stop flow or damage lines. Inspect in daylight from safe access; never prime, repair or approach a suspect moraine during a storm or outburst warning. A dry outlet is a fault, not evidence of a safe lake.
- Start with a closed hydraulic flume and geotechnical review (years 0–3), then monitored noncritical reservoir trials (years 3–7); only consider authorized glacial-lake pilots and comparison with conventional mitigation over years 7–20. Retrieve pipe, anchors and valves for reuse/recycling; do not leave fragments in the watershed.
