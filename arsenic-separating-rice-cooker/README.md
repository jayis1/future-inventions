# Arsenic-Separating Rice Cooker

**Author: jayis1**

**Category:** Food Safety & Universal Basic Services / Rice Preparation

**Status:** TRL 2 research concept; not a certified food-safety intervention

## Problem

Inorganic arsenic in rice can add to exposure from contaminated drinking and cooking water; long-term exposure is associated with cancer and other harms. WHO emphasizes that providing a safe water supply is the first priority in affected communities, and reports that millions of people are exposed to elevated arsenic in groundwater.[2] Ordinary absorption cooking retains the water and any dissolved arsenic in the food. Published parboil-and-absorb and percolation methods can remove part of rice's inorganic arsenic *when the cooking water itself is safe*; the percentages vary by grain and method and must not be assumed for every household.[1][3]

## Solution

A repairable, stovetop **two-stage rice cooker** separates the first, arsenic-bearing blanch water from the grains before the finishing water can enter. It has a 304-stainless perforated grain basket in a 304-stainless inner pot, a bottom-drain channel with a removable, gasketed 304-stainless waste vessel, and a physically separate, covered finishing-water chamber. A keyed gravity gate for the finishing water can open only when the first-water vessel is seated beneath the drain and a drain-completion float has latched; a visible volume mark makes an incomplete drain obvious. The sealed vessel is then removed for cooling and disposal, and removal resets the latch before the next batch. The gate is *not* an arsenic sensor. The cook first blanches the rice in pre-boiled, independently verified low-arsenic potable water, drains the liquor into the closed vessel, adds fresh verified safe water and cooks by absorption on an ordinary stove. A simple insulated outer jacket reduces heat loss without claiming to eliminate the energy needed to heat water. Components disassemble without proprietary cartridges, electricity or connectivity.

**Pilot design envelope, not demonstrated performance:** up to 0.5 kg dry white or brown rice per batch; 2.0 L of safe blanch water plus up to 1.0 L of safe finishing water per batch (actual finishing volume must be tuned by rice type); a five-minute blanch trial followed by complete drainage and ordinary absorption cooking. Proposed *test gate*: at least 40% reduction in inorganic arsenic mass per dry-rice batch against matched absorption-cooked controls, with no batch asserted to meet a food standard without laboratory speciation. A pilot must also measure cooked yield and nutrient retention; if those deteriorate materially, abandon or revise the design. Three litres of independently safe water per batch is a resource requirement, not a guaranteed consumption figure.

## Key Innovation

Parboil-and-absorb and percolating-water arsenic reduction are **published prior art**, not inventions claimed here: one study reported 54% and 73% inorganic-arsenic removal for tested brown and white rice using parboil-and-absorb, while another found variable results across tested grains using percolation.[1][3] The unproven system contribution is a **physically separated, inspectable dirty-water capture and fresh-water handoff** that prevents the blanch liquor being accidentally reabsorbed during an interrupted cook. Unlike the repository's arsenic groundwater-treatment network, this tackles arsenic already inside harvested rice rather than removing arsenic from a well; unlike its paddy water-table gate, it operates after harvest, at cooking. No worldwide first or patent novelty is claimed.

A handoff prototype must demonstrate that neither an unseated waste vessel nor a plugged drain permits the finishing-water gate to open, even if the stove cools or the operator walks away. The clean-water chamber must have no shared liquid path with the waste vessel. The mechanical interlock is a design hypothesis, not a proof of chemical safety; the user must still use separately tested safe water for *both* stages.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 USD engineering target at 10,000 units/year: US$45 ex-workshop per 0.5 kg-batch cooker**, comprising a stainless pot/basket, removable waste vessel, gravity gate, mechanical latch, gasket and insulation jacket. This is not a vendor quote; the metal grade and food-contact approvals require sourcing checks. Target service life is five years with a replaceable US$3 gasket annually, subject to leak, corrosion and thermal-cycle testing. At one batch/day for 300 days/year, straight-line capital allocation plus one gasket is US$12/year or **US$0.04/batch**, excluding safe water, fuel, cleaning, labor, shipping, taxes, assays, permitted waste handling and certification. A pilot should meter delivered heat and aim for ≤2.5 MJ/batch at the pot on the specified 0.5 kg/3 L duty; the target may fail with a small stove or long cook. If extra water or fuel costs exceed the local benefit compared with a lower-arsenic rice source and safe-water provision, the device is not an appropriate intervention.

## Impact

At one passing batch per day for 300 days, one cooker could process at most **150 kg dry rice/year**; that is a capacity scenario, not a measured exposure or health benefit. A proposed 100-household, one-year trial would compare the same rice lots under normal absorption cooking, established parboil-and-absorb instructions without this hardware, and this device. Independently measure raw and cooked inorganic arsenic by speciation, arsenic in discarded liquor, water arsenic and microbial quality, nutrient loss, metered fuel and water, incomplete drains, spills, operator time and total cost. Failure to outperform a clearly taught existing cooking method in *real use*, or an inability to provide safe water and a lawful drain pathway, ends the pilot. A 10–20-year route is a small engineering pilot, replicated independent trials across rice varieties and water qualities, then local food-contact approval if warranted—not a claim of immediate benefit at scale.

**Safety boundary:** This device does not remove arsenic *from cooking water* and cannot turn contaminated water into potable water. High-arsenic or microbiologically unsafe water can make food less safe; a qualified supply and local food-safety advice take precedence.[2] The blanch liquor must be contained, cooled and disposed of through a locally approved wastewater route, not poured into drinking wells or kitchen gardens; the pilot must assess actual effluent arsenic mass and legal disposal options. A leak, boiling-dry, scald, blocked drain, gate bypass or missing vessel stops use until serviced. Do not serve a batch as verified low-arsenic food merely because the handoff mechanism worked; lab testing, nutrient and microbiological assessments are separate. Avoid collecting household identities or meal records.
