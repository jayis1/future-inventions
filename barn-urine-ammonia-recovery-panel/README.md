# Barn Urine Ammonia Recovery Panel

**Author: jayis1**

**Status: research concept; performance, cost and field safety are unvalidated**

## Problem

Urine mixed with feces or left exposed in animal housing can lose nitrogen as ammonia (NH₃). The lost nutrient cannot then be used on the farm, and agricultural air pollution affects communities beyond the farm.[1][2] This proposal addresses **already source-separated urine in covered collection channels** at eligible barns, not emissions from manure lagoons, fields, or whole-barn exhaust. It does not justify increasing herd size or reducing required ventilation.

## Solution

A removable panel seals over a shallow, existing urine-only collection channel before the first storage tank. A washable coarse screen catches bedding; a replaceable **expanded-PTFE (ePTFE) gas-permeable tube cassette** hangs in the contained headspace above the urine, without touching it. A small peristaltic pump circulates a metered dilute sulfuric-acid solution inside the tube into a closed, labelled, secondary-contained HDPE receiver. As microbial urease hydrolyses urea, some dissolved ammonium becomes gaseous NH₃; that NH₃ diffuses through the hydrophobic membrane and reacts inside the receiver: `2 NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄`. Membrane contact with acid and pig slurry has been studied, but this urine-channel installation and its performance have **not** been tested.[1]

The urine exits through the farm's normal enclosed drain to its existing lawful manure-management route. A mechanically unobstructed overflow protects the channel even with a blocked screen or failed pump. A sealed receiver, drip tray, visible leak strip, local low-flow flag and service log permit inspection without cameras, animal identification or a network connection. Only trained service personnel exchange acid/product cartridges; farmers must not refill an open acid tank. Where acid supply, trained handling or lawful product disposal are absent, do not install this device.

## Key Innovation

**Neither membrane ammonia capture nor ammonium-sulfate fertilizer is new.** Pig-slurry ePTFE experiments used acid circulated inside tubing and found that membrane placement, pH and aeration affect measured recovery; short laboratory results do not establish performance in a urine channel.[1] The proposed contribution is a *source-side, service-exchangeable panel* that intercepts volatilization before urine reaches storage while preserving an open hydraulic overflow and an isolated chemical circuit. It is distinct from this repository's `ventilation-safe-nitrogen-scrubber`, which treats diluted, mechanically exhausted **air** with an acid spray and ventilation bypass; this panel treats the **covered source-separated liquid pathway** using a membrane, not the ventilation duct. Compare against both an uncovered channel and an equally covered channel without a membrane so that a benefit from the cover is not misattributed to the cassette. Existing membrane manure systems are prior art, not evidence of patent novelty.

**Unvalidated pilot target:** at a site delivering 100 L/day source-separated urine at 1 g total ammoniacal nitrogen (TAN)/L *after hydrolysis*, recover 30 g N/day in the receiver, with at least 30% less measured NH₃-N emitted from the channel than an equally covered control over a 30-day service period. These are independent endpoints: recovery alone cannot prove avoided emissions, and 30 g/day is conditional on the stated feed TAN, temperature, pH, contact time and membrane area. Size membrane area by measured mass-transfer flux in a prototype, not an assumed laboratory removal percentage; do not add alkali or aerate urine simply to inflate NH₃ release. Low pH or low temperature could make the target impossible.

For that *hypothetical* 30 g N/day recovery, stoichiometry requires at least **0.105 kg pure H₂SO₄ equivalent/day** and yields at most **0.142 kg dry ammonium sulfate equivalent/day** before impurities, dilution and losses. A sealed 20 L receiver initially charged with 1 N H₂SO₄ has a theoretical neutralization capacity of **0.280 kg N** (normality in equivalents/L), enough for about nine days at that illustrative recovery rate, but the **service target is seven days** with reserve and independent pH/titration checks. This is not a promise that 100 L of urine contains that much recoverable NH₃. Receiver volume, acid compatibility, operating pH and final fertilizer concentration require laboratory verification before manufacture.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 design target, at 1,000 panels/year: US$450 ex-workshop** per 100 L/day nominal channel module: replaceable ePTFE cassette US$130, HDPE sealed receiver/bund US$80, corrosion-compatible pump/tubing US$75, recycled-PP cover/screen/overflow US$65, leak/flow indicators US$30, assembly and leak test US$70. Installation survey and drainage modifications are provisionally **US$150–400/site**, not included; nor are taxes, delivery, electricity, laboratory product assays, acid, trained weekly exchange, membrane replacement or hazardous-waste fees. At the illustrative continuous-load point, pure-acid equivalent alone is about **38 kg/year** per unit; reagent delivery, servicing and membrane fouling could dominate capital cost. Obtain site quotes and compare lifetime cost per kilogram *verified recovered* N with source separation, ordinary covers, and incumbent manure treatment. A cooperative cartridge route might spread service cost; affordability is not demonstrated.

## Impact

An eligible panel could retain nitrogen that would otherwise be lost while reducing local NH₃ release, but **no measured benefit or health outcome is claimed**. Ten panels at the illustrative design point would recover at most **109.5 kg N/year** if each maintained that feed and recovery every day; real uptime, TAN formation, acid logistics and the covered-control emissions comparison determine the result. The WHO identifies agricultural air pollution as affecting both rural and more distant populations; source control is only one part of a wider management strategy.[2] This design is only useful if the net life-cycle burden of acid production, transport, power, failed cartridges and disposal is smaller than the avoided harm. Captured N is not permission to overapply fertilizer; check nutrient balance and local rules.

## Safety, maintenance and validation

- **Acid leak or membrane wetting:** Secondary containment and a visible leak indicator trigger pump shutdown, drain isolation from the chemical loop and trained spill response. Test liquid cross-contamination by sulfate/conductivity in urine and organic markers in receiver; do not release either stream as fertilizer after a suspected breach. Hydrophobicity loss and fouling are known risks in manure membranes.[1][3]
- **Power loss, blocked screen or pump failure:** The channel overflow preserves drainage and the existing barn ventilation remains untouched. Stop chemical circulation on low flow or power loss, flag the unit as *not capturing*, and direct urine through the normal contained route; never claim emissions control in bypass. Clean the screen and inspect gasket/overflow at each exchange.
- **Sensor drift, variable pH and missed service:** Check pH and acid reserve by independent titration at cartridge exchange; log urine flow, TAN, temperature, receiver N and channel headspace NH₃ flux. A saturated receiver is sealed and collected, not topped up by an untrained user. Missing or conflicting measurements mean unknown recovery, not success.
- **Product and disposal:** Analyse every receiver batch for free acid, N and applicable contaminants before any lawful fertilizer use; dispose of unqualified solution and contaminated screens through approved routes. Return ePTFE tubing and HDPE/PP components for segregated recycling where practical. Never spray, inhale, or open the receiver in occupied animal housing.
- **Falsification trial:** Over two warm/cold seasons, randomize matched urine channels among uncovered, covered-only and covered-plus-panel treatments at the *same* collection practices and ventilation. Measure time-integrated NH₃-N flux, influent/effluent TAN and urine volume, receiver N, acid consumed, leaks, worker exposure, power and service labor; reconcile a nitrogen balance with stated uncertainty. Stop for any unsafe drainage, chemical escape, increased worker exposure or worse emissions than the covered control. Advance only if the emission and receiver targets both hold, fertilizer batches meet local standards and an independent chemical/animal-housing safety review approves the site. A 10–20 year deployment horizon assumes these gates and does not imply approval today.

## How It Works

The panel is a retrofit for a **urine-only channel that already drains safely**, not a device placed beneath mixed bedding. Urine flows under the covered cassette; a screen diverts solids without narrowing the independent overflow. Urease gradually converts urea to ammoniacal nitrogen. Only the free NH₃ fraction—not NH₄⁺ directly—can cross the ePTFE tube into recirculating acid. Protonation inside the tube keeps the NH₃ partial pressure low, while the sealed receiver accumulates ammonium sulfate. An acid-reserve check precedes each cartridge exchange; spent liquid stays contained until it passes product-quality testing. Remaining urine follows the existing drainage route. No caustic addition or forced aeration is assumed: if natural pH and residence time leave too little free NH₃, the cassette fails its performance gate rather than changing the barn's emissions profile to make recovery look better.[1]

## Technical Architecture

`Urine-only inlet → screened covered channel → existing drain/overflow`; in parallel, `channel NH₃ headspace → suspended ePTFE tubing → closed acid loop → sealed receiver`. Recycled polypropylene forms the removable cover and screen; the ePTFE cassette provides the gas-selective interface; acid-compatible tubing, a peristaltic pump and an HDPE secondary bund isolate the reagent from animals and runoff. A low-flow flag and leak strip stop circulation and call for trained service, while the passive drain stays open. Record inlet/outlet volume and TAN, receiver nitrogen, acid reserve, temperature, pH, power and service hours. The audit balance is **inlet total N = outlet total N + receiver N + measured gaseous N + other measured losses ± inventory change**, all in grams of N over the same interval; TAN alone omits urea and organic N. Size membrane area only after a measured NH₃-N flux (g N/m²/day) under representative cool and warm conditions. Literature slurry-chamber capture cannot be transplanted into this headspace geometry without a test.[1]

## Performance Benchmarks

These are **unmeasured acceptance targets**, not product specifications or claims of superiority. At the illustrative 100 L/day and 1 g TAN/L after hydrolysis (100 g TAN/day), a 30-day prototype should put at least 30 g N/day in its receiver on valid operating days, while its **time-integrated channel NH₃-N emissions are at least 30% lower than a matched, equally covered control**. Compare to an uncovered channel separately to isolate the cover's effect. Report recovery as receiver N / incoming TAN and emissions as g NH₃-N/channel/day with paired temperature, pH, flow and ventilation; do not equate the two percentages. Aim for ≥80% measured availability and a seven-day cartridge route, with **zero detectable acid-to-drain leaks** by pre-specified conductivity/sulfate tests; record kWh/kg recovered N, acid kg/kg N and technician minutes/kg N rather than inventing efficiency values. At the target point the stoichiometric acid floor is 3.50 kg pure H₂SO₄ per kg captured N, before excess or losses. Published pig-slurry laboratory configurations achieved 36–72% NH₃-N removal and reported 9.8–21.2 €/kg N recovered; those are **different feedstocks, boundary conditions and cost scopes**, not barn-channel baselines or validated targets for this module.[1]

## Deployment Scenarios

- **Single eligible barn:** One existing urine-separation line with about 100 L/day throughput hosts a panel alongside a covered-only control. A trained technician swaps the closed receiver weekly; the operator checks the overflow and leak flag daily. No installation where separation or safe cartridge handling is absent.
- **Cooperative pilot:** Ten eligible channels across farms share a cartridge collection route and laboratory assays. Compare matched channels in warm and cold seasons before claiming lower emissions; at the continuous design point ten units would capture 109.5 kg N/year, but 80% availability would reduce that illustrative figure to 87.6 kg/year.
- **New-build housing:** Specify accessible urine-only drains, a permanently clear bypass and external chemical-service access at design time. Integrate with local manure permits and fertilizer rules without changing required barn ventilation.

## Risks & Mitigations

- **Weak transfer or seasonal variation:** Urea conversion, pH, dilution and cold weather may keep free NH₃ low. Log feed composition and temperature, measure flux and reject sites below the paired-control gate rather than adding alkali or aeration.
- **Fouling and blocked drainage:** Bedding and mineral deposits can clog the screen or wet the membrane. Provide a visible clear bypass, inspect at every exchange, and track flux decline and clean/replacement intervals; a blockage or leak stops the trial.
- **Chemical exposure and contaminated product:** Isolate the acid circuit in a bund, stop pumping on leak or low flow, and restrict exchange to trained staff with site-approved handling procedures. Assay each receiver for free acid, N and locally regulated contaminants; unapproved batches go to lawful disposal, never a field.
- **Economics and displaced harm:** The US$450 workshop target excludes service, acid and assays. A farm should compare total cost and life-cycle impacts against a simple cover and incumbent manure management; recovered N alone is not evidence of avoided NH₃ or net climate benefit.

## Vision for 2050

If independent trials demonstrate durable reductions *beyond a cover alone*, safe cartridge logistics and affordable recovery, urine-separating barns could treat source-side nitrogen as a measured nutrient stream rather than an invisible loss. Cooperatives might exchange standardized cassettes and return verified ammonium sulfate to local nutrient plans. This is a conditional future, not a prediction: unsuitable barns keep their existing drainage and ventilation, and the technology earns its place only where monitored air quality, worker safety and full-cycle costs justify it.
