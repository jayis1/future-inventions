# Batch-Locked Gasifier Cooktop — Impact Analysis

**Author: jayis1**

**Status: illustrative scenario arithmetic, not measured impact or a carbon-credit estimate.** The cooktop has not been built or certified. Read the [README](README.md) and [roadmap](ROADMAP.md) first. WHO identifies household air pollution as a major burden, but its population-level mortality estimate cannot be apportioned to this device.[1]

## Boundary and assumptions

- Horizon: one operating year after a hypothetical 1,000-unit deployment; low/central/high sustained-use fractions are 30%/60%/80% of units sold. Inactive units contribute zero displaced cooking, although their manufacturing footprint remains.
- Active unit: two eligible 0.5 kg batches per day on 300 days/year; each batch displaces the *same useful cooking task* on the household's existing biomass appliance. Stacking or fewer suitable meals lowers actual displacement.
- Proposed pellet lower heating value: 15 MJ/kg; proposed full-cycle stove efficiency: 25%. Assumed counterfactual fuelwood lower heating value: also 15 MJ/kg and counterfactual useful-pot efficiency: 15%. These are scenario inputs, **not measured site-specific baselines**; actual moisture, char credit, cookware and operating phases change the result.
- Fan energy: 3 Wh per nominal one-hour batch; charger losses and battery replacements are excluded. Cost goal: US$85 bare cooker at 100,000 units/year plus estimated US$30–50 battery/charger, excluding installation, CO alarm, ventilation, distribution, fuel, maintenance, taxes and end-of-life treatment.
- Comparison: useful heat = batches × 0.5 kg × 15 MJ/kg × 0.25. Equivalent baseline wood = useful heat ÷ (0.15 × 15 MJ/kg). Pellet mass = batches × 0.5 kg. A kilogram of residue pellet is not ecologically interchangeable with a kilogram of harvested wood.

For the central case, 360,000 × 0.5 kg = 180 tonnes of pellets; 360,000 × 1.875 MJ = 675 GJ at the pot; 675 GJ ÷ 0.15 ÷ 15 MJ/kg = 300 tonnes of equivalent baseline wood. The 120-tonne *mass difference* is not a carbon saving, a reduction in deforestation, or a cost saving. Ex-factory aspiration for 1,000 units is US$85,000 (or US$115,000–135,000 with the estimated battery/charger), before every excluded cost. Five-year life is an unverified design assumption, not a depreciation or warranty claim.

## Exposure, climate and uncertainty

The proposed pilot target is ≥30% lower *measured personal PM₂.₅* with no rise in CO, versus the household baseline; 1,000 sales do not imply 300–800 healthier households. Exposure reduction is not equivalent to a 30% reduction in disease, deaths or DALYs. Measure kitchen and personal exposure, background sources and stacked-stove use before making a health projection. WHO's clean-cooking definition requires actual emissions performance, not a fuel label.[1]

Net CO₂-equivalent change is deliberately **unquantified** pending fuel sourcing, fraction of nonrenewable biomass, pellet drying and transport energy, methane/black-carbon emission factors, manufacturing, battery replacement and end-of-life data. Audit gross wood displacement (150–400 t/year *under the assumptions above*) separately from lifecycle climate accounting. Do not book biochar storage or carbon credits without measured char yield, stability, soil fate and an applicable verified methodology. Likewise, do not imply zero emissions because the fuel is biomass.

The strongest sensitivity is sustained use: halving central active use halves modeled fuel throughput and useful heat, but not factory cost or embodied impact. If real full-cycle efficiency is below 25%, useful heat and equivalent displacement fall proportionally at fixed batches; if the baseline exceeds 15% efficiency, displacement falls. Pellet supply failure, wet fuel, an unsafe latch or increased CO can turn apparent benefit into harm and should halt deployment. Values are planning scenarios for supervised research, not forecast adoption or tested performance.
