# Brownout-Safe Cooking Rail

**Author: jayis1**
**Status: research concept (TRL 2); not a certified appliance or electrical installation.**

## Problem

WHO estimates that about 2.1 billion people still cook with polluting fuels; household air pollution was associated with an estimated 2.9 million premature deaths in 2021.[1] Solar mini-grids can support electric cooking, but evening peak loads and weak supply can interrupt meals. A Tanzania DC-cooking pilot reported battery depletion during cloudy periods and damaged connectors following repeated shutdown/reconnection.[2] An unfinished meal can push a household back to a smoky stove. This proposal targets shared kitchens and already-electrified mini-grid communities, not homes without an affordable electricity supply.

## Solution

A **three-port, 48 V nominal DC cooking rail** accepts up to three purpose-built, low-voltage **250 W resistive insulated pots**, fed through a locally isolated 1 kW-rated supply with a verified 48 V battery input. A fused, current-limited distribution board measures bus voltage and each port current; simple local controllers coordinate heating in short time slots. An independent hardware undervoltage cutoff disconnects all heating before the battery's protection system trips. The cook selects a locally labeled *heat-now* or *hold-warm* setting; during scarcity, the system allocates a minimum heating quota fairly across occupied pots, never silently marks food cooked, and visibly announces insufficient power. There is no remote shutoff, identity record, camera, or internet requirement.

Each pot uses a replaceable food-contact stainless inner liner, mineral-wool insulated outer sleeve, resistive element bonded beneath the liner, mechanical temperature limiter, lid vent, and detachable keyed connector. The rail is an appliance for a supervised shared kitchen, not a substitute for licensed wiring, safe food handling or a battery-management system. It has **no new energy source**: users must have adequate solar-plus-storage or clean grid capacity. An underpowered evening session must be stopped, not assumed safe because the pot feels warm.

## Key Innovation

Electric cooking, DC power hubs, demand management and appliance load limiting are established art.[2][3] The proposed, testable integration is **meal-aware graceful degradation without networked control**: a replaceable port module gives each pot a bounded, auditable heat allocation while a separate hardwired undervoltage trip avoids damaging repeated deep discharges. Local lamps show heating, paused and insufficient-energy states; the controls do **not** infer microbial food safety from elapsed time. A head-to-head test must establish whether this arrangement completes more meals without connector failures than a conventional protected multi-pot electric kitchen on the *same* constrained supply. Unlike the repository's [Solar Heat Cassette Cooker](../solar-heat-cassette-cooker/), this supplies simultaneous live electric heat rather than transporting a charged thermal cartridge; unlike the [Batch-Locked Gasifier Cooktop](../batch-locked-gasifier-cooktop/), it burns no biomass at point of use.

**Proposed operating targets, not measured results:** three 250 W ports on a regulated 48 V bus; total heat draw ≤750 W, excluding converter losses; each port allocated at least 100 W *when* 300 W of usable electrical power is available and all three are active; automatic heater disconnection before the qualified battery undervoltage limit; ≥1,000 connector mating cycles after splash and dust testing. On a 250 W port, two hours at rated draw consumes 0.5 kWh electrical before supply losses. Neither the thermal energy delivered to food nor adequacy for a particular recipe is established by that number. A 2 L water load heated from 20°C to 95°C needs roughly 0.174 kWh ideally (2 kg × 4.186 kJ/kg/K × 75 K ÷ 3,600); vessel losses, cooking ingredients and holding time increase actual demand.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 goal at 10,000 kitchen rails/year:** US$300 ex-workshop for a three-port rail (US$70 isolated power interface and protection, US$80 bus and three replaceable port modules, US$60 enclosure/connectors, US$90 assembly and safety test), plus **US$65 per insulated pot**. Thus US$495 for one rail and three pots *before* the battery, PV, electrical installation, freight, taxes and certification. Budget separately for an already-qualified mini-grid supply, spare connectors, yearly inspection and recycled electronics; do not present US$495 as a complete installed clean-cooking system. At 30 households sharing one rail, the equipment-only goal is US$16.50/household before capacity, scheduling, service and financing; uptake and cooking throughput may make that sharing ratio impractical. Quote real local alternatives (LPG, standard electric pressure cookers and mini-grid upgrades) before funding a pilot.

## Impact

The potential benefit is fewer abandoned electric meals and fewer returns to polluting fuel, **conditional on measured displacement**, not a forecast of lives saved. A 10-kitchen randomized crossover pilot would compare completed meals, fallback fuel use, personal/kitchen PM₂.₅ and CO, bus brownouts, connector temperatures, electricity used per fully cooked meal, safety incidents and cook satisfaction against a standard protected electric-cooking setup on equal supply. An engineering target is ≥90% of planned qualifying meals completed without an unsafe shutdown when the power budget is met; test under clouds, simultaneous starts and high-demand recipes. A 10–20-year route to deployment is power-electronics qualification, recipe trials with local cooks, electrical/food-contact certification, and service training, not an assumed breakthrough in battery chemistry. Later community adoption could reduce smoke exposure, but cannot be credited until sustained polluting-stove displacement is observed.

**Safety and limits:** A low bus voltage is not intrinsically safe at high current. Each port needs independent fusing, short-circuit and earth-fault protection appropriate to the installation, insulation checks, keyed dry-break connectors, strain relief and accessible hot-surface warnings. A stuck relay, absent pot, sensor disagreement, overtemperature, reversed wiring, wet connector, or controller failure must de-energize the affected heater; independent thermal cutouts protect against a software fault. Loss of power leaves food partially cooked: the display defaults to *not complete* and users follow local food-temperature guidance or discard unsafe food. A cooked-state claim must never be generated from heating time alone. Test faults with qualified electricians before occupied-space trials. Replace liner and port modules locally; recycle copper, steel and electronics and manage spent batteries under local rules. Avoid proprietary cookware lock-in. The concept does not authorize appliance deployment or prove health benefit.

## How It Works

A cook seats a dry, keyed pot connector, selects *heat-now* or *hold-warm*, and explicitly starts a session. The proposed 48 V input is a qualified, current-limited isolated supply connected to an existing battery system; the rail does not charge or protect that battery by itself. Each 250 W heater draws about 5.2 A at 48 V when on, so three simultaneous heaters demand about 15.6 A, before control and conversion losses. In each short scheduling window, the controller measures bus voltage and port currents, then grants heating slots up to the available input budget. A 100 W average quota for each of three active pots is only possible if at least 300 W remains available for heating; a quota is not a promise that a recipe will finish. If the supply cannot sustain a safe cooking schedule, an unmistakable *insufficient energy / meal incomplete* indication replaces the quota.

A separately powered or fail-open undervoltage interlock removes heater power before the *specific qualified battery system's* low-voltage cutoff; its threshold and hysteresis must be set from that system's documented limits, not a universal 48 V number. After a trip, the controller records no cooked-state assertion; voltage recovery requires a stable dwell and a deliberate user restart, rather than unexpected reheating. Thermal limiters act independently of the scheduling software. A fuse limits port fault energy but is not a substitute for appropriate DC-rated interruption, wiring protection, or a qualified installer.

## Technical Architecture

The proposed path is **isolated source → input protection and hardwired undervoltage contactor → fused 48 V copper bus → three replaceable switched port modules → bonded resistive heaters**. Isolated voltage/current sensing reports to a local scheduler; the scheduler requests port switching but cannot override the series undervoltage interlock or each pot's mechanical thermal cutoff. Local lamps distinguish energized, paused, faulted, and meal-incomplete states; fault counters can remain on-device with no user identity or connectivity. Stainless liners carry food, mineral wool limits heat loss, and keyed splash-resistant connectors separate the wet cooking zone from energized contacts. Exact creepage, enclosure, DC arc ratings, ingress resistance, and grounding/earthing scheme require design review for the installation's supply topology.

Design validation must inject open and shorted sensors, stuck-on switching, welded contactors, reversed connections, controller resets, wet connectors, and oscillating input. A single failed semiconductor is not a safe disconnect: an independent series device and thermal limiter must interrupt heating, with fault detection preventing restart; welded-device detection and actual fault-clearing capability remain unproven. On control-power loss the requested state is off, not automatically resumed. Any fault that cannot be safely isolated disables the whole rail pending service.

## Performance Benchmarks

| Metric and comparable baseline | Proposed acceptance target, **not measured** | Verification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Completed qualifying meals vs. a protected three-pot electric kitchen on the same constrained supply | ≥90% when the agreed energy budget is met; demonstrate an improvement over comparator rather than assume one | Randomized crossover; identical recipes, pot load, available energy, and interruptions; log fully cooked outcomes and fallback fuel |
| Connector durability vs. the pilot comparator's installed connectors | ≥1,000 mating cycles without unsafe resistance rise or contact exposure | Cycle under specified rated current, dust/splash conditioning, and temperature-rise measurement; publish comparator's measured result |
| Undervoltage protection vs. the battery maker's BMS trip | Heater disconnect before BMS trip under tested sag profiles; no uncommanded restart | Calibrated voltage/current logging and fault injection at full 750 W heater demand |
| Supply and meal energy | 750 W maximum aggregate heater nameplate; 0.5 kWh electrical for one port at 250 W for two hours, **not** a measured meal requirement | Meter at rail input and pot terminals; account separately for conversion losses and incomplete meals |

There is no verified state-of-the-art performance number for an equivalent three-port weak-grid rail in the present sources; the protected comparator must be measured in the same pilot. Source reports describe real mini-grid constraints, not this device's achieved performance.[2][3] Test at defined battery state of charge, ambient temperature, recipe mass, supply impedance, and repeated low-voltage events; report nuisance trips, hot connectors, and unsafe failures alongside completion rate.

## Deployment Scenarios

1. **Supervised mini-grid community kitchen:** three cooks share a qualified 48 V supply during an evening peak. A local operator schedules portions and checks that each meal reaches its recipe-specific safe endpoint; the rail displays shortages rather than pretending a warm pot is a cooked meal.
2. **Clinic or school auxiliary kitchen:** where existing electrical capacity and approved food handling already exist, staff use locally serviced insulated pots to defer nonurgent heating during a sag. Critical medical circuits must remain separate; this is not a backup power system or a food-safety certification.
3. **Solar-plus-storage cooking cooperative:** a trained technician installs the rail behind appropriate protection and measures actual battery capacity and evening loads before admitting three simultaneous users. Persistent deficits require more generation/storage, fewer loads, or a different cooker—not a software workaround.

## Risks & Mitigations

Brownouts can cause relay chatter and battery deep-discharge; a hardware trip with hysteresis, stable-recovery dwell and manual restart is the proposed defense, to be demonstrated under fault injection. Welded switches, sensor drift and controller reboot could energize a heater unexpectedly; independent interruption, thermal cutouts, diagnostic disagreement lockout, and accessible isolation must be verified, not assumed. A 48 V bus at high current can overheat a loose connector or sustain a DC arc; use rated contactors, per-port protection, strain relief, enclosure tests and scheduled connector replacement. Wet handling, burns, incorrect cookware, blocked vents, and improvised bypasses require keyed connectors, clear hot-surface and incomplete-meal warnings, local-language training and repairable approved parts. A stalled, partially cooked meal is a food-safety risk; staff must check food temperature against local guidance and discard where necessary. The price excludes supply upgrades, batteries and service; require installed-cost quotes and independent electrical/food-contact review before any occupied-space pilot. Battery disposal and replacement affect lifecycle impact and belong in procurement plans.

## Vision for 2050

If qualified power, affordable service, and actual displacement of smoky cooking can be established, small community kitchens could allocate scarce evening electricity transparently rather than hiding outages behind an unreliable heating indicator. By 2050 a repairable rail might be as ordinary as a protected distribution board: locally maintained, interoperable with different approved pots, and honest about when a meal cannot finish. This is a conditional adoption scenario, not a prediction of universal access or a substitute for building sufficient generation and storage.
