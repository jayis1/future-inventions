# Capillary Salt-Export Tile

**Author:** jayis1

**Category:** Food Security & Soil Stewardship / Localized Farm Salinity

**Status:** Research concept (TRL 2); performance and economic viability unverified.

## Problem

FAO estimates that 1,381 million hectares of land worldwide have salt-affected soils, including roughly 10% of irrigated and 10% of rainfed cropland; those figures include sodic soils and natural saline ecosystems, not all suitable for treatment.[1] On small, already bare, salt-affected farm margins, evaporation can leave soluble salts where new crops germinate. Freshwater leaching needs drainage and suitable water; moving saline water without collecting its salts merely relocates the problem. The goal here is a *small-plot experiment* for salt export where conventional drainage is impractical, not a cure for all saline farmland or for sodicity.

## Solution

Install a replaceable **unfired-clay/ceramic porous wick** through a shallow slot in a *fallow, non-flooded* saline strip, with its lower end approximately 10–20 cm deep and its upper end inside a **covered, raised evaporation cassette**. A mineral-compatible HDPE basin with a removable lid and insect-screened vapor openings keeps condensed rain out of the wick mouth and retains any precipitated salt. The wick contacts a replaceable, porous ceramic spreader above a removable polypropylene liner; capillary flow wets the spreader, and its evaporating porewater draws more water upward while dissolved ions are left in the cassette. The spreader must present enough exposed wetted area to sustain the measured evaporation target; its 0.1 m² plan area alone does not establish that flux. Salt is not destroyed. Operators close the vapor openings and remove the cassette before heavy rain, weigh its dry solids, and send captured material to an approved local saline-waste route (or an assayed, permitted recovery process); never spread it on another field or discharge it into freshwater. No electricity, additives, organisms, or telemetry are required.

**Unverified design envelope:** one 0.1 m² evaporation cassette over a 10 m² trial strip; sandy-loam to loam soil with an accessible wet pore-water pathway, no shallow saline water table and no crop roots crossing the installation slot. The ceramic is a 30 cm-long, 20 mm-wide replaceable porous strip; geometry, pore size and capillary conductivity must be measured before field use. Exclude clay that cannot resupply the wick, dry soils, flood-prone plots, and fields with contaminated salts until independently assessed. A dry wick exports nothing; no water is added merely to make the device work.

## Key Innovation

The experiment couples a **subsurface wick to a rain-isolated, removable and weighable salt collector at field-strip scale**. Wick evaporation and salt crystallization are established physical processes, not new discoveries. Unlike the repository's [Electrokinetic Soil Revival Array](../electrokinetic-soil-revival-array/), this proposal uses no electric field or microbial treatment and does not claim whole-hectare desalination. Unlike the [Coastal Lens Recharge Gate](../coastal-lens-recharge-gate/), it removes soluble salt from an already saline soil strip rather than adding approved rainwater to an aquifer. The falsifiable question is whether salts exported *off site*, after subtracting airborne deposition and soil-surface salt redistribution, exceed the maintenance and moisture costs of a passive wick. It cannot correct sodium-induced soil structural damage; sodic sites need separate diagnosis and management.

A transparent proposed mass balance is `salt recovered (g/day) = source water reaching spreader (L/day) × dissolved-solids concentration (g/L) − salt lost to leakage/dust − salt retained in the ceramic`. For a **hypothetical** 0.2 L/day evaporation from the cassette fed at 5 g/L, the upper-bound export is 1 g/day per cassette, or 0.1 g/m²/day averaged over a 10 m² strip; 120 operational days would at most export 120 g from that strip. This is *not* a measured yield, nor proof of a meaningful reduction in root-zone salinity. Weigh collected solids and separately assay ions and soil salt inventories at fixed depths to close the balance. Rain entry, capillary limits, salt crust sealing the pores and replenishment by saline groundwater can make net benefit zero or negative.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 research goal, at 10,000 locally assembled units/year:** US$35 ex-workshop per 0.1 m² cassette (US$12 HDPE enclosure and rain lid; US$8 replaceable ceramic wick/spreader; US$5 removable liner and screen; US$10 assembly), plus US$15 installation allowance per 10 m² strip. Allow US$8/unit-year for replacement wick/liner and cleaning labor, excluding soil and waste assays, transport, permitted salt disposal, land, taxes, field survey and flood protection. Thus 100 cassettes would nominally cover 1,000 m² at US$5,000 initial installed cost *before* monitoring and disposal: it is not an affordable hectare-wide remediation promise. Compare a full paid-season cost per gram of **verified net root-zone salt removed** against locally engineered drainage, irrigation changes and salt-tolerant crops before any expansion. Ceramic can be locally formed, HDPE repaired/recycled where facilities exist; contaminated ceramic and liners require characterized waste handling.

## Impact

The candidate addresses an agricultural problem affecting millions of livelihoods, but no yield gain or beneficiaries are claimed. In a permitted 10–20-year path, first test paired wicks and covered cassettes in soil columns, then compare 30 randomized 10 m² fallow strips with 30 adjacent sham-cassette strips over two dry seasons. Pre-register a **prototype target** of at least 0.5 g/day dry soluble salt collected per working cassette and a statistically distinguishable decline in 0–20 cm soluble-salt inventory relative to matched controls, without greater soil-water depletion, reduced neighboring crop yield, or unacceptable waste-disposal costs. The collected mass must be corroborated by ionic analysis and water/salt budgets; a salty crust without a soil-inventory decline is failure. Record rainfall, soil moisture, groundwater level, soil texture, electrical conductivity and laboratory chloride/sulfate. EC alone cannot prove salt export.

A first disproof experiment is a 90-day column test with equal initial saline soil and moisture in paired columns: if the wick column does not show a measured decline in total soluble-salt inventory *and* recovered cassette ions account for that loss within analytical uncertainty, stop development. Shut or lift cassettes before storms; spills or broken lids mean isolate, recover salt and re-test the soil. Avoid saline runoff, dust exposure during dry-salt handling and attracting salt to vulnerable crop roots. If trials succeed, limit deployment to surveyed, consented strips with funded maintenance and a legal salt-disposal route; larger landscape benefits remain conditional, not forecast.

## How It Works

A 10–20 cm-deep pore-water contact feeds the replaceable ceramic wick. Evaporation from its porous spreader establishes a water-potential gradient; dissolved ions travel with that water and crystallize on the spreader or fall onto a removable liner inside the covered HDPE cassette. The cassette is a collector, not a salt sink: brush or replace the spreader over the liner, assay and weigh recovered solids, seal them for an approved waste route, and keep a transfer log. No measurable export occurs until salts actually leave the site. The effective capillary lift depends on pore radius, wetting and hydraulic conductivity: a fine pore may lift water but deliver too little flux, while a coarse pore may not reach the spreader. Bench testing must select a pore-size/flow compromise, not infer performance from dimensions alone.

On each service cycle, record pore-water volume and ion concentration, collected dry salt and ion composition, ceramic-retained salt, leakage, rain entry and soil-water change. `incoming dissolved salt = recovered + ceramic-retained + leaked + unaccounted` is the cassette boundary; compare treated and sham 0–20 cm soil inventories to test whether the recovered mass originated in the target strip rather than neighboring soil, airborne dust or saline groundwater. Rain can redissolve crust and reverse the flow, so shut the cassette before storms and treat a flooded unit as a failed cycle. Conventional leaching also requires a route that carries salty drainage out of the treatment area; collection, not evaporation alone, is the differentiator here.[2]

## Technical Architecture

**Soil interface → ceramic wick → porous spreader → lidded HDPE cassette → removable polypropylene liner → characterized waste.** The buried wick and spreader are replaceable; the screened vent passes water vapor but is not a guaranteed rainproof or dustproof seal. An inspection tag records installation depth, liner exchange, seal condition and waste transfer; field instruments for the *trial* (soil-moisture probes, rainfall gauge, sampled porewater and depth-resolved laboratory soil assays) are not part of the proposed passive product. The data flow links paired-strip soil inventories, water flux and recovered ion mass by cassette ID and date. A removable splash lip and secondary containment are design candidates to test against wind-driven rain, not proven safeguards. HDPE, polypropylene and candidate ceramic must pass salt-exposure, UV and wet–dry durability tests before procurement.

## Deployment Scenarios

1. **Fallow farm margin:** a dry-season 10 m² saline strip next to a field, only where moisture and sampling access permit paired sham controls; avoid crop roots and check neighboring crop water stress.
2. **Saline greenhouse walkway:** a non-food-production edge with managed irrigation and accessible cassette exchange; reject sites with contaminated salts or runoff to drains until waste characterization and containment are approved.
3. **Community demonstration plot:** a surveyed, non-flooded plot used for a supervised soil-column-to-field comparison, with a named operator and prearranged legal salt disposal; educational value is not evidence of agronomic recovery.

## Risks & Mitigations

- **Pore clogging and crystallization:** record daily flux and exchange the ceramic/spreader at a pre-registered decline threshold; test wet–dry, freeze–thaw and abrasion cycles rather than assuming a service life.
- **Water stress and salt redistribution:** compare depth-resolved soil inventory and moisture with sham strips and adjacent crop plots; stop if salt is merely concentrated at the surface or crop moisture falls materially.
- **Rain intrusion, dust and back-diffusion:** inspect lids after storms, close vents, collect spills under containment and assay soil after each breach; invalidate any cycle without a defensible salt balance.
- **Waste and material failure:** characterize co-concentrated contaminants, provide sealed handling and approved disposal, and assay ceramic/polymer leachate; refuse deployment where take-back, worker protection or disposal capacity is absent.

## Vision for 2050

If independent paired trials show durable net root-zone export at an acceptable full lifecycle cost, surveyed fallow strips could use repairable cassettes as one *small-area* option alongside drainage, water management and salt-tolerant planting. Operators would track collected ions to permitted destinations rather than count visible crust as progress. This is a conditional design vision, not a forecast of global reclamation, crop-yield gains or adoption.
