# Coastal Lens Recharge Gate

**Author: jayis1**

**Category:** Groundwater Resilience & Universal Basic Services / Coastal Salinity

**Status:** Research concept (TRL 2); not a drinking-water treatment or authorization to recharge an aquifer.

## Problem

In coastal Bangladesh, approximately 20 million people face saltwater-intrusion threats to surface and groundwater sources, according to UNICEF.[1] Over-pumping, storm-surge contamination and changing recharge can erode a shallow freshwater lens. Adding water at the wrong place or of uncertain quality can instead contaminate wells. UNICEF already describes village-scale pond/roof-water managed aquifer recharge (MAR) in Bangladesh,[1] and UNESCO describes MAR as established practice that can help protect against seawater intrusion.[2] The missing research question is whether a *small, source-isolated and locally observable* recharge interface can improve lens salinity outcomes at suitable sites without making water quality or neighbors' supplies worse.

## Solution

A surveyed community roof drains through a first-flush bypass and removable coarse screen into a covered **5 m³ food-grade HDPE rainwater tank**. After laboratory-approved source-water checks, a trained cooperative operator moves a captive three-position valve from **bypass** to **recharge**. The tank drains by gravity into a shallow, lined, accessible sand-and-gravel gallery above an identified *unconfined* sandy coastal aquifer, with an open overflow back to the permitted drainage route. The gallery infiltrates through the native unsaturated zone; it is not an injection well or a drinking-water filter. No gallery is installed where the unsaturated zone, geology, flood elevation, groundwater rights or contamination risk fails hydrogeologist and regulator review.

Two screened observation points—one between coast and gallery, one inland near the protected supply zone—are sampled for groundwater level and electrical conductivity (EC), with periodic laboratory chloride, microbial, nitrate and site-specific contaminant tests. EC is an imperfect salinity proxy, not proof of potability or a unique tracer of seawater. Record rainfall, actual tank drawdown, bypass volume and nearby pumping. During cyclone warnings, source contamination, overtopping, missing quality results, unexpected groundwater chemistry or gallery clogging, **bypass and isolate the tank**; continue ordinary independent well testing and safe-water provision. No automatic well shutoff, remote telemetry or person-tracking is involved.

## Key Innovation

Neither rainwater MAR nor freshwater hydraulic barriers are new.[1][2] The proposed *research delta* is a **reversible, above-ground gravity interface** combining physical first-flush/source isolation with *paired coast-to-inland groundwater observations and an explicit bypass state*: it tests whether a cooperative can distinguish mere surface infiltration from a verified freshwater-lens benefit before authorizing more installations. The [Alternating Recharge Terrace](../alternating-recharge-terrace/) tests two serviceable surface beds for storm-flow infiltration in water-scarce catchments; this proposal tests coast-to-inland salinity and head response from a covered roof-rain supply and a single gallery. The [Flood-Isolating Wellhead Collar](../flood-isolating-wellhead-collar/) excludes floodwater at an existing well; this device does not seal or treat a well. A valve or sensor alone is not a novel groundwater mechanism; the integration and its measurable coastal outcome are unproven.

**Unmeasured design envelope:** one eligible 100 m² roof, 5 m³ covered tank, one inspected gallery, and a minimum 0.5 m unsaturated separation at *seasonal high* water table only if local authorities accept that separation (a greater setback may be required). A 100 mm rain event on 100 m² yields 10 m³ before losses; at an assumed 0.8 runoff coefficient, 8 m³ reaches the conveyance *before* first-flush diversion and tank overflow. Tank capacity caps any one empty-tank event at 5 m³. These are volume bounds, not aquifer recharge or salinity performance claims; rainfall seasonality, roof condition, soil permeability and downstream water allocations determine viability. Do not pressurize the gallery to meet a flow target.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 goal at 1,000 locally assembled sites/year:** US$2,500 site hardware and local assembly allowance: covered HDPE tank and lid US$850; roof conveyance, screen and first-flush bypass US$220; valve and overflow US$230; gallery liner, sand and graded gravel US$380; two protected observation points and manual level/EC kit US$420; local assembly and commissioning US$400. A community cooperative or public water authority pays; keep service manuals and replaceable fittings open to local suppliers. This is *not* an all-in bid: hydrogeological survey, borehole drilling where costly, land, permits, accredited chemistry/microbiology, cyclone-resistant civil works, taxes, finance and any alternative safe-water service are excluded. Budget an additional unquoted US$250–600/site-year for paid cleaning, calibration, sampling and waste handling, plus locally quoted lab costs. If those costs exceed the public benefit, use established supply protection or demand reduction instead.

## Impact

At **20 fully eligible empty-tank fills per year**, the arithmetic ceiling is `20 × 5 = 100 m³/site-year` leaving the tank; 1,000 sites imply at most 100,000 m³/year entering galleries, *not* net aquifer storage, safe drinking water, 20 million beneficiaries or a predicted retreat of the saline front. Repeated partly full tanks, rejected events, evaporation, lateral leakage and groundwater flow lower effective benefit. Any coastal salinity improvement would be measured against a matched existing-MAR control and changes in pumping, rainfall, tide and storm exposure, not attributed to the gate by default.

Over a 10–20-year development horizon, first test source isolation, gallery infiltration and fail-to-bypass behavior in a non-potable pilot, then run a two-wet-season, independently reviewed comparison of 20 permitted sites against 20 hydrogeologically matched conventional roof/pond MAR sites. Pre-register a target of **≥10% lower dry-season chloride concentration at inland observation points** than matched controls, with **no increase in microbial or chemical exceedances, no adverse neighboring-well level change and no reduction in protected downstream flow**. These are *unproven research gates*, not safe-water or climate-benefit claims. Use hydrogeological modeling and tracer/ion fingerprints to distinguish dilution from real front movement. Fail the concept if baseline variability masks the signal or if maintenance, source quality, or water rights cannot be sustained. Scale only under local MAR and drinking-water regulation, with independent potable-well testing; the existing safe-water supply remains in service during trials.

## Safety and end of life

- Floodwater, roof contamination or a missing laboratory result means no recharge; a screen and sand do not remove dissolved hazards or guarantee pathogen safety. Protect the tank and observation bores against cross-contamination and test all water under an approved plan.
- Clogging, tank leaks, damaged covers and overflow obstruction require manual bypass, emptying or repair. The overflow is sized by a site engineer for flood hydraulics and must never divert a design storm onto neighbors; no sealed pressure injection is permitted.
- EC drift or a single salty observation is inconclusive: calibrate with standards and confirm chloride in a lab. Reassess tidal variation, over-pumping, upconing and borehole leakage before attributing a change to the sea or the gallery.
- Keep sediment, used filters and contaminated gallery media contained for locally approved disposal. Reuse or recycle HDPE and metal where safe; monitor water and material withdrawals so the scheme does not transfer scarcity downstream.

## How It Works

The gate uses a short, inspectable path rather than a buried injection system. A surveyed roof supplies rain to a first-flush bypass; later runoff passes a removable debris screen into the covered 5 m³ HDPE tank. The operator checks the source-water approval record, flood warning and tank condition before opening the captive valve to the gallery. Gravity head—not a pressurized pump—moves accepted water through the sand-and-gravel gallery and native unsaturated sand. A separately engineered open overflow carries rejected and excess water back to a permitted drain. The gallery is lined where needed to prevent uncontrolled lateral discharge at the surface, but its infiltration face must remain open to the approved soil; a fully sealed gallery could not recharge anything.

Fresh rain entering an unconfined coastal aquifer *might* raise local freshwater head and change salinity at an inland observation point. It might instead drain laterally, mix with brackish water, mound under a neighbor's property or mobilize contaminants. Water-level, EC and laboratory chloride series at the coastward and inland points, together with rainfall, tide and pumping logs, test those alternatives. Operators revert to bypass upon a failed quality check, anomalous chemistry, flooding or blocked gallery; a site engineer must ensure the bypass/overflow remains safe even when recharge is isolated. A measured drop in EC alone is not proof that an additional cubic metre of drinkable water exists.

## Technical Architecture

- **Source and isolation:** surveyed roof → first-flush diverter → screen → opaque, covered food-grade HDPE tank → captive manual bypass/recharge valve. Keep the tank and gallery accessible for cleaning; protect vents and fittings from insects, floodwater and corrosion.
- **Hydraulic path:** valve → shallow sand-and-gravel gallery → unsaturated native sediment → aquifer; the independent open overflow returns to an approved drainage route. Verify soil infiltration and separation at seasonal high groundwater before sizing the gallery; no injection pressure or connection to a drinking-water well.
- **Evidence path:** manual tank-level and rainfall records estimate admitted volume; two screened observation points record hydraulic head and temperature-compensated EC; an accredited lab checks chloride, microbiology, nitrate and site-specific hazards. Compare against matched conventional MAR sites and baseline tidal/pumping variation, not a single before/after snapshot.
- **Control and accountability:** trained cooperative operators keep dated valve, maintenance, sampling and bypass logs; an independent hydrogeologist reviews trends and neighboring-well effects. The physical valve works without power or connectivity. The authority can suspend recharge while existing safe-water service and well tests continue.

## Performance Benchmarks

These are **unmeasured engineering bounds and research gates**, not results. The state-of-practice comparator is a locally permitted roof/pond managed-aquifer-recharge installation with matched geology, pumping and source-water treatment; rainwater MAR already exists in coastal Bangladesh.[1] There is no defensible universal infiltration or chloride baseline across coastal aquifers.

| Measure (one eligible site) | Existing practice / comparison | Proposed target or bound; test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Runoff admitted from a 100 mm event on a 100 m² roof | Site-specific conventional MAR; measure its actual captured volume | 10 m³ rainfall × assumed 0.8 runoff = 8 m³ before diversion/overflow; at most 5 m³ into an initially empty tank. Meter tank drawdown and bypass. |
| Annual water leaving tank | Meter the matched MAR controls | At most 100 m³/year at 20 fully eligible 5 m³ fills; fewer fills or rejected events reduce it. This is **not** retained groundwater. |
| Inland dry-season chloride | Two-wet-season matched controls, adjusted for tides and pumping | Pre-registered ≥10% lower concentration than controls, with no chemical/microbial exceedance increase; assess laboratory chloride and uncertainty, not EC alone. |
| Neighboring head and protected downstream flow | Seasonal baseline and matched controls | No adverse neighboring-well level change or reduction in protected flow; stop and investigate any attributable harm. |
| Energy and service | Measure all electricity, labor and sampling for both approaches | Gravity transfer has no designed pump energy; record actual ancillary energy and paid service. US$250–600/site-year is an **unquoted goal**, excluding lab fees, and is not validated by the MAR source. |

The cost and water comparison must include hydrogeological survey, permits, testing, upkeep and replacement; the US$2,500 hardware/local-assembly goal is not a delivered-water price. An observed chloride improvement can coexist with inadequate potable quality or no recoverable yield.

## Deployment Scenarios

1. **Coastal Bangladesh community roof:** a cooperative with an eligible, uncontaminated roof and an existing safe-water supply tests one gallery upgradient of monitored supply wells. Compare against a matched conventional roof/pond MAR site for two wet seasons; no household supply switch is inferred from a good EC reading.
2. **Cyclone-exposed barrier island:** an authority keeps the gate isolated through overwash and tests roof, tank and groundwater chemistry after the event. Only after floodwater and source quality are independently cleared does a permitted pilot resume; the device cannot prevent the original surge from salinizing the lens.
3. **Small coastal utility catchment:** several eligible roofs could be evaluated under one water-rights and monitoring program where soil, setbacks and downstream drainage allow it. Stagger operation to attribute responses and stop if neighboring wells mound or protected flows decline; a non-qualifying site uses existing supply protection instead.

## Risks & Mitigations

| Failure mode | Detection and response |
| --- | --- |
| Dirty roof runoff, pathogens or dissolved contaminants | First flush and screen are only pretreatment; reject on failed or missing laboratory result, isolate the tank and use an approved source-water plan. Do not claim potable treatment. |
| Saltwater intrusion, mixing or pumping-induced upconing | Trend paired heads and lab chloride alongside tide and abstraction logs; suspend recharge and investigate before attributing a change to the gate. Limit pumping independently. |
| Clogging, biofilm or unsuitable geochemistry | Track tank drawdown and ponding; bypass, clean accessible media, sample precipitates and reassess aquifer compatibility before restart. |
| Groundwater mounding or storm overflow onto neighbors | Model wet-season high water and survey drainage rights; isolate recharge and route overflow through the engineer-approved drainage path if limits are reached. |
| Sensor drift, bore leakage or operator error | Calibrate EC against standards, seal and inspect observation bores, cross-check with independent labs and signed manual logs. Missing data means bypass, not optimistic interpolation. |
| Flood damage, changing sea level or loss of land access | Locate above mapped flood levels where feasible; maintain a storm isolation procedure, periodic site reassessment and a cooperative maintenance/land agreement. Withdraw the site if these cannot be sustained. |

## Vision for 2050

If replicated matched trials show a durable chloride benefit without compromised water quality or neighbors' rights, locally assembled gates could become one *optional* element of coastal aquifer stewardship: small utilities would know exactly which clean-roof events they admitted, which they rejected and whether the inland lens actually responded. Atolls and deltas would still need pumping limits, protected rainwater storage, independent drinking-water testing and sometimes desalination. The strongest outcome is not a universal salinity cure but a public, auditable decision to install only where monitored benefit exceeds the full cost and risk.
