# Coastal Lens Recharge Gate

**Author: jayis1**

**Category:** Groundwater Resilience & Universal Basic Services / Coastal Salinity

**Status:** Research concept (TRL 2); not a drinking-water treatment or authorization to recharge an aquifer.

## Problem

In coastal Bangladesh, approximately 20 million people face saltwater-intrusion threats to surface and groundwater sources, according to UNICEF.[1] Over-pumping, storm-surge contamination and changing recharge can erode a shallow freshwater lens. Adding water at the wrong place or of uncertain quality can instead contaminate wells. UNICEF already describes village-scale pond/roof-water managed aquifer recharge (MAR) in Bangladesh,[1] and UNESCO describes MAR as established practice that can help protect against seawater intrusion.[2] The missing research question is whether a *small, source-isolated and locally observable* recharge interface can improve lens salinity outcomes at suitable sites without making water quality or neighbors' supplies worse.

## Solution

A surveyed community roof drains through a first-flush bypass and removable coarse screen into a covered **5 m³ food-grade HDPE rainwater tank**. After laboratory-approved source-water checks, a trained cooperative operator moves a captive three-position valve from **bypass** to **recharge**. The tank drains by gravity into a shallow, lined, accessible sand-and-gravel gallery above an identified *unconfined* sandy coastal aquifer, with an open overflow back to the permitted drainage route. The gallery infiltrates through the native unsaturated zone; it is not an injection well or a drinking-water filter. No gallery is installed where the unsaturated zone, geology, flood elevation, groundwater rights or contamination risk fails hydrogeologist and regulator review.

Two screened observation points—one between coast and gallery, one inland near the protected supply zone—are sampled for groundwater level and electrical conductivity (EC), with periodic laboratory chloride, microbial, nitrate and site-specific contaminant tests. EC is an imperfect salinity proxy, not proof of potability or a unique tracer of seawater. Record rainfall, actual tank drawdown, bypass volume and nearby pumping. During cyclone warnings, source contamination, overtopping, missing quality results, unexpected groundwater chemistry or gallery clogging, **bypass and isolate the tank**; continue ordinary independent well testing and safe-water provision. No automatic well shutoff, remote telemetry or person-tracking is involved.

## Key Innovation

Neither rainwater MAR nor freshwater hydraulic barriers are new.[1][2] The proposed *research delta* is a **reversible, above-ground gravity interface** combining physical first-flush/source isolation with *paired coast-to-inland groundwater observations and an explicit bypass state*: it tests whether a cooperative can distinguish mere surface infiltration from a verified freshwater-lens benefit before authorizing more installations. The [Alternating Recharge Terrace](../alternating-recharge-terrace/) tests two serviceable surface beds for storm-flow infiltration in water-scarce catchments; this proposal tests coast-to-inland salinity and head response from a covered roof-rain supply and a single gallery. The [Flood-Isolating Wellhead Collar](../flood-isolating-wellhead-collar/) excludes floodwater at an existing well; this device does not seal or treat a well. A valve or sensor alone is not a novel groundwater mechanism; the integration and its measurable coastal outcome are unproven.

**Unmeasured design envelope:** one eligible 100 m² roof, 5 m³ covered tank, one inspected gallery, and a minimum 0.5 m unsaturated separation at *seasonal high* water table only if local authorities accept that separation (a greater setback may be required). A 100 mm rain event on 100 m² yields 10 m³ before losses; at an assumed 0.8 runoff coefficient, 8 m³ reaches the conveyance *before* first-flush diversion and tank overflow. Tank capacity caps any one empty-tank event at 5 m³. These are volume bounds, not aquifer recharge or salinity performance claims; rainfall seasonality, roof condition, soil permeability and downstream water allocations determine viability. Do not pressurize the gallery to meet a flow target.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 goal at 1,000 locally assembled sites/year:** US$2,500 site hardware and local assembly allowance: covered HDPE tank and lid US$850; roof conveyance, screen and first-flush bypass US$220; valve and overflow US$230; gallery liner, sand and graded gravel US$380; two protected observation points and manual level/EC kit US$420; local assembly and commissioning US$400. A community cooperative or public water authority pays; keep service manuals and replaceable fittings open to local suppliers. This is *not* an all-in bid: hydrogeological survey, borehole drilling where costly, land, permits, accredited chemistry/microbiology, cyclone-resistant civil works, taxes, finance and any alternative safe-water service are excluded. Budget an additional unquoted US$250–600/site-year for paid cleaning, calibration, sampling and waste handling, plus locally quoted lab costs. If those costs exceed the public benefit, use established supply protection or demand reduction instead.

## Impact

At **20 fully eligible empty-tank fills per year**, the arithmetic ceiling is `20 × 5 = 100 m³/site-year` leaving the tank; 1,000 sites imply at most 100,000 m³/year entering galleries, *not* net aquifer storage, safe drinking water, 20 million beneficiaries or a predicted retreat of the saline front. Repeated partly full tanks, rejected events, evaporation, lateral leakage and groundwater flow lower effective benefit. Any coastal salinity improvement would be measured against a matched existing-MAR control and changes in pumping, rainfall, tide and storm exposure, not attributed to the gate by default.

Over a 10–20-year development horizon, first test source isolation, gallery infiltration and fail-to-bypass behavior in a non-potable pilot, then run a two-wet-season, independently reviewed comparison of 20 permitted sites against 20 hydrogeologically matched conventional roof/pond MAR sites. Pre-register a target of **≥10% lower dry-season chloride concentration at inland observation points** than matched controls, with **no increase in microbial or chemical exceedances, no adverse neighboring-well level change and no reduction in protected downstream flow**. These are *unproven research gates*, not safe-water or climate-benefit claims. Use hydrogeological modeling and tracer/ion fingerprints to distinguish dilution from real front movement. Fail the concept if baseline variability masks the signal or if maintenance, source quality, or water rights cannot be sustained. Scale only under local MAR and drinking-water regulation, with independent potable-well testing; the existing safe-water supply remains in service during trials.

## Safety and end of life

- Floodwater, roof contamination or a missing laboratory result means no recharge; a screen and sand do not remove dissolved hazards or guarantee pathogen safety. Protect the tank and observation bores against cross-contamination and test all water under an approved plan.
- Clogging, tank leaks, damaged covers and overflow obstruction require manual bypass, emptying or repair. The overflow is sized by a site engineer for flood hydraulics and must never divert a design storm onto neighbors; no sealed pressure injection is permitted.
- EC drift or a single salty observation is inconclusive: calibrate with standards and confirm chloride in a lab. Reassess tidal variation, over-pumping, upconing and borehole leakage before attributing a change to the sea or the gallery.
- Keep sediment, used filters and contaminated gallery media contained for locally approved disposal. Reuse or recycle HDPE and metal where safe; monitor water and material withdrawals so the scheme does not transfer scarcity downstream.
