# Cotton–Polyester Fiber Refinery

**Author:** jayis1

**Status:** Research concept (TRL 2); no performance or cost claims validated

**Development horizon:** 10–20 years for independently validated cooperative-scale modules

## Problem

Cotton–polyester garments are difficult to recycle into comparable textiles: mixed fibers are hard to separate mechanically, while dye, elastane and finishes complicate chemical recovery. Discarded clothing consumes landfill capacity and displaces demand for virgin fibers. Textile-waste burdens extend across global collection and manufacturing supply chains affecting millions of workers and residents; this device would only address a qualified fraction of that stream.

## Solution

A cooperative-scale, enclosed **0.5 dry tonne/day pilot target** accepts pre-sorted, labeled cotton/PET fabric (initial study window: 40–80% PET by dry mass). Garments are inspected to remove hardware; a supervised sampling assay checks fiber composition, dyes and finishes. Elastane-rich, coated, PVC-containing and unknown batches are refused, not blended into a good batch. Clean reuse and conventional mechanical recycling take precedence whenever fiber quality permits.

1. Cut and wash the selected fabric; capture lint and treat wash water separately. Recycle wash water only after a batch-specific quality check.
2. Expose PET surfaces with controlled cutting and aqueous agitation, avoiding a blanket claim that the entire fabric must be melted or that enzymes work equally well on crystalline PET. Treat in a jacketed, insulated vessel at a **research envelope of 55–75 °C and pH 7–9** with replaceable PET-hydrolase enzyme; test residence time and enzyme loading rather than assuming a fixed yield. PET ester-bond hydrolysis consumes water and produces terephthalic acid (TPA) and ethylene glycol (EG), with intermediate oligomers to be measured.
3. Filter and wash the remaining cellulose-rich solids. Qualify their viscosity, contamination and yield for a pulp or regenerated-cellulose buyer; do not call damaged fibers reusable yarn. Separate TPA by controlled acidification and filtration, then assay TPA purity and recover EG from the aqueous phase only if separation energy and economics justify it. Route unrecovered EG and dissolved organics to licensed wastewater treatment, never a drain.
4. Record dry input, added water/chemicals, all product and reject streams, and a batch-specific uncertainty interval. Ship neither cellulose nor TPA as a product without independent contaminant and quality tests.

**Pilot gates, not observed results:** on qualified feed, PET conversion ≥70% (moles of PET repeat units yielding assayed TPA, including sampling uncertainty), ≥80% of incoming cellulose dry mass recovered as assayed usable pulp, net fresh water ≤5 m³ per dry tonne of feed, and metered electricity plus purchased heat ≤500 kWh-equivalent per dry tonne. Pilot at multiple dye types and PET crystallinities; compare against sorted-fiber mechanical recycling and conventional disposal with identical collection boundaries. Failed gates mean redesign or no deployment.

## Key Innovation

The proposed advance is **feed-qualified, cellulose-preserving PET hydrolysis with separate quality gates for both output streams**, rather than claiming a universal enzyme that processes mixed rubbish. PET hydrolases and textile enzymatic processing are prior art; the research hypothesis is that selective PET conversion can leave a saleable cellulose pulp while a small, serviceable module keeps a closed, audited contaminant and water balance. This differs from this repository's [textile dye recovery system](../closed-loop-textile-dye-recovery-system/) (dyehouse wastewater chemistry) and [solar-polymer upcycler](../solar-polymer-upcycler/) (mixed plastics and high-temperature catalysis). It is not a claim of worldwide novelty or patentability.

**Bounding mass balance:** For a hypothetical 100 kg *dry, clean* 60/40 PET/cotton feed, the pilot gates imply at least 42 kg of the original PET repeat-unit mass enters the reacted fraction and at least 32 kg of original cellulose is recovered as usable pulp; as much as 18 kg of unconverted PET and 8 kg of cellulose can still require reprocessing or disposal at those threshold yields. The hydrolysis products do **not** weigh just 42 kg: water contributes atoms (PET repeat unit C₁₀H₈O₄ + 2 H₂O → TPA C₈H₆O₄ + EG C₂H₆O₂). Report TPA, EG, soluble intermediates, residual solids, added water and contaminants on a measured whole-process balance; dyes and finish residues make real batches less tidy than this illustration.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted design target:** ≤US$250,000 installed per 0.5-dry-tonne/day module at 100 units/year by 2040, including enclosure, vessel, washing, filtration, controls, commissioning and wastewater containment. Target operating expense ≤US$500 per dry tonne accepted, including electricity, heat, enzyme replacement, water, assays, maintenance and residue disposal but **excluding** inbound collection/sorting and outbound freight; report these separately before making any competitiveness claim. Assume 10-year capital life with annual independent service and replaceable pumps, seals and enzyme cartridges; neither enzyme lifetime nor the prices of recovered products are established. Small cooperatives should be able to buy processing as a service, rather than fund the plant upfront.

## Impact

If validated and placed near existing textile sorting hubs, modules could reduce disposal of eligible blends and demand for virgin PET/cellulose inputs; no net climate or water benefit is asserted until a cradle-to-gate lifecycle comparison includes sorting, enzyme production, heat, water treatment, residuals and actual displacement of virgin material. Local repair and laboratory partnerships are part of the adoption pathway; workers must be trained and paid to reject unsuitable batches. Regions without wastewater treatment, independent assays or reliable energy may not be suitable until those services exist.

## Safety, limitations and validation

- **Wrong feed or toxic finishes:** misclassification can contaminate products or poison enzymes. Test representative batches for fiber content and restricted substances; quarantine uncertain lots and send rejects to an approved recycler or licensed handler.
- **Hot liquid, pH adjustment and leakage:** insulated guarded vessels, bunding, leak detection, pressure relief and operator PPE are required. Loss of power or a failed level/temperature sensor halts feeding and keeps the batch contained; no automatic safe-product indication is inferred from a single sensor.
- **Enzyme aerosol and biological exposure:** use contained liquid handling and verified cleaning procedures; do not release concentrated enzyme mist. Replace spent reagents under occupational-safety controls.
- **Wastewater and off-spec solids:** monitor COD, EG, dyes and site-specific hazardous residues. Contain and transfer off-spec streams to permitted treatment; never describe residues as harmless compost. Product qualification does not substitute for discharge permits.
- **Falsification:** independent trials on diverse real garments must close a dry-solids and chemical mass balance within a prespecified measurement uncertainty, establish stable pulp specifications and TPA purity, measure enzyme replacement frequency and all utilities, and beat an equivalent local mechanical-recycling/disposal comparator in both lifecycle impact and fully burdened cost. If high crystallinity, dye removal, residual disposal or enzyme expense prevents this, the concept does not pass its deployment gate.
