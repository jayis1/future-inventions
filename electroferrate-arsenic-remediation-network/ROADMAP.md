# Electroferrate Arsenic Remediation Network — Roadmap

Five phases from concept to ubiquity, focused on real field reliability rather than paper performance.

---

## Phase 1 — Concept & Bench Validation (2026–2028)
The first phase proves the chemistry stack under controlled but realistic groundwater conditions. Bench reactors validate ferrate generation efficiency on NiFeOx anodes, As(III) oxidation kinetics across pH 6.3–8.0, arsenate capture on magnetite-laterite granules, and repeatable electro-regeneration without rapid scaffold degradation. Synthetic groundwater matrices include phosphate, silicate, iron, manganese, and organic matter to avoid the classic mistake of optimizing only in clean lab water. The waste path is validated in parallel: arsenic-rich concentrate is blended into Fe-As-P ceramic pellets and tested for TCLP leachability, mechanical durability, and safe handling. **Exit criteria:** <5 µg/L effluent in 90% of test runs from 50–500 µg/L influent, >95% As(III) oxidation in under 2 minutes, 50 regeneration cycles with <20% capacity loss, TCLP-compliant pelletized residue.

## Phase 2 — Prototype Engineering (2028–2030)
A ruggedized household unit and a 1 m³/day community prototype are built around the validated chemistry. Focus shifts from performance peaks to survivability: pump fouling, electrode cassette replacement, low-cost sensors, battery buffering, flow control, and intuitive user interfaces. The prototype is designed for local manufacturing where possible, using molded housings, commodity DC electronics, and regionally sourced laterite or iron-rich aggregate. Verification workflows are standardized with strip-reader imaging and monthly independent lab checks. **Exit criteria:** six-month continuous operation in controlled outdoor testing, <$250 household BOM and <$2,000 community BOM at pilot scale, maintenance tasks executable by non-engineer operators in under 30 minutes.

## Phase 3 — Field Pilots & Service Model (2030–2033)
The system enters the environments that matter: arsenic-affected villages, schools, and clinics. Pilots in South Asia, Latin America, and one private-well market test not only treatment performance but adoption, payment, trust, and waste custody. A local service network is developed to handle regeneration consumables, sensor verification, and pellet collection. Partnerships with public-health agencies and water NGOs create a compliance and monitoring framework that communities can actually sustain. **Exit criteria:** at least 30 pilot sites across 3 geographies, >95% uptime, >85% user retention after one year, zero uncontrolled disposal events, independently verified drinking-water compliance.

## Phase 4 — Scale & Standardization (2033–2040)
Manufacturing expands from pilot batches to regional production. Standardized electrode cassettes, modular control boards, and sorbent cartridge geometries reduce cost and simplify training. District-level stabilization hubs handle pelletization and waste tracking for hundreds to thousands of household nodes. Financing models mature: school procurement, utility-fringe leases, village co-op ownership, and public-health subsidies where cancer-prevention economics justify support. **Exit criteria:** 100,000+ deployed units, household water cost consistently below $0.10/m³, regional manufacturing in at least 4 countries, audited waste chain in routine operation.

## Phase 5 — Ubiquity & Public Infrastructure (2040–2050)
EARN-class treatment becomes boring in the best way: a standard part of safe-water infrastructure in arsenic-prone regions. New wells are commissioned with integrated treatment, schools treat arsenic compliance like electricity or refrigeration, and ministries track arsenic-safe coverage in national dashboards. Local manufacturing and service ecosystems make systems repairable without foreign dependence, while decades of epidemiological follow-up show measurable declines in arsenic-related disease burden. **Exit criteria:** millions of people served, treatment embedded in public procurement standards, pelletized arsenic waste managed as a routine hazardous-material stream, and arsenic exposure no longer accepted as a normal feature of rural life.
