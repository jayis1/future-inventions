# Impact Analysis — Electromineral Pipe Regenerator

## Purpose
This analysis estimates the public-health, cost, and infrastructure effects of deploying EPR as a bridge technology in buildings with lead service lines, lead solder, or legacy lead-bearing fixtures.

## Core Assumptions
- Target deployment window: 2030–2045
- Primary use cases: single-family homes, schools, clinics, multifamily buildings, and emergency municipal response
- Treated building lifetime before major retreatment: **3–10 years** depending on chemistry stability and plumbing disturbance
- Home treatment cost: **$800–2,500**
- Institutional treatment cost: **$8,000–40,000**
- Maintenance node cost: **$180–500** for homes, **$1,500–6,000** for larger buildings
- Lead reduction target after treatment: **70–95%** relative to pretreatment first-draw conditions
- Dissolved lead capture during active rehab: **>95%**

## Reference Performance Envelope
| Parameter | Low case | Mid case | High case |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| First-draw lead reduction | 70% | 85% | 95% |
| Buildings treated per crew-day | 1 | 2 | 4 |
| Useful treatment duration | 3 years | 5 years | 10 years |
| Home-equivalent cost | $2,500 | $1,400 | $800 |
| Maintenance energy | 0.02 kWh/m3 | 0.01 kWh/m3 | 0.005 kWh/m3 |

## Health Impact Logic
Lead exposure has no known safe level for children. Even when a full pipe replacement program exists, people remain exposed during the waiting period. EPR's main value is compressing that waiting period by years.

If a city has **100,000** lead-risk residences and can replace only **5,000–10,000** per year, a large share of households may wait **10+ years** for excavation-based resolution. If EPR reaches even **20,000** of the highest-risk buildings in the first 3 years, the population-level reduction in chronic exposure could be substantial.

## Scenario A: Mid-size city rapid-response program
Assumptions:
- **20,000 homes** treated
- Average cost per home: **$1,400**
- Average pretreatment first-draw lead: **18 ppb**
- Post-treatment average: **3 ppb**
- Mean reduction: **15 ppb**

Outputs:
- Program cost: **$28 million**
- If full replacement would otherwise occur 8 years later on average, the city gains roughly **160,000 home-years** of reduced exposure before excavation catches up
- For a household using **2 liters/day** of tap water for direct consumption, that is about **10.95 grams less lead-equivalent exposure per billion liters consumed** citywide relative to the pretreatment baseline; the exact biological uptake depends on age and diet, but the exposure direction is decisively improved

## Scenario B: School district intervention
Assumptions:
- **150 schools** treated
- Cost per school: **$18,000**
- Average of **40** drinking/cooking outlets per school
- 90% of outlets brought below **5 ppb** first-draw lead

Outputs:
- Program cost: **$2.7 million**
- Protected outlets: about **6,000**
- Summer-break deployment could eliminate years of bottled-water procurement, emergency filter replacement labor, and disruption to students and staff

## Scenario C: Multifamily housing portfolio
Assumptions:
- **300 buildings**
- Mean treatment cost: **$14,000**
- Average residents per building: **36**

Outputs:
- Program cost: **$4.2 million**
- Residents receiving improved water quality: about **10,800**
- Lower wall-opening and excavation burden than full near-term repiping, which matters in occupied affordable housing

## Cost Positioning
EPR is not a permanent substitute for pipe replacement, but it can be economically attractive when compared with:
- repeated faucet-filter distribution and replacement,
- bottled-water logistics during crises,
- emergency fixture shutdowns in schools and clinics,
- deferred public-health damage during long replacement queues,
- partial repiping that leaves hidden lead sources in place.

A home-level treatment at **$1,400** that remains effective for **5 years** implies about **$280 per protected home-year** before financing. For many municipalities, that is competitive with emergency workaround programs alone.

## Environmental Impact
### Positive
- Large reduction in disposable filter cartridge waste
- Small hazardous waste stream concentrated into recoverable service canisters instead of distributed household trash
- Much lower material intensity than excavating and replacing every at-risk line immediately
- Low operational energy demand relative to most water-treatment hardware

### Cautions
- Precursor chemicals must be tightly dosed to avoid overfeeding phosphate or causing loose precipitates
- Captured lead concentrate requires certified handling and recycling
- Poorly targeted deployment could divert attention from the need for permanent line replacement

## System-Level Effects
At scale, EPR could shift utilities from passive corrosion control to active distributed asset rehabilitation.

Potential system benefits:
- Faster triage after water-chemistry disturbances
- Better prioritization data for eventual excavation
- Reduced inequity between neighborhoods that can and cannot self-fund interim protection
- More transparent compliance evidence through onsite verification logs

## 2045 Deployment Thought Experiment
If by 2045 EPR or comparable systems reach:
- **10 million homes globally** at an average of **$1,100** each, and
- **500,000 institutional buildings** at an average of **$15,000** each,

then gross deployment would total roughly:
- Homes: **$11.0 billion**
- Institutions: **$7.5 billion**
- Combined: **$18.5 billion**

That is large, but still plausible compared with the economic burden of lead exposure, bottled-water crises, and delayed infrastructure replacement across multiple decades.

## Bottom Line
EPR's strongest impact is time. It turns a slow, capital-heavy, excavation-bound public-health fix into a same-day or same-week building intervention. If it reliably pushes most treated taps below **5 ppb**, captures released lead safely, and maintains performance for multiyear intervals, it could protect millions of people during the long transition toward fully lead-free plumbing.
