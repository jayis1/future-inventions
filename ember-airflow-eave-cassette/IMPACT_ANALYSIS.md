# Ember-Airflow Eave Cassette — Impact Analysis

**Author: jayis1** · Illustrative 10-year scenarios, not observations, forecasts or a claim of wildfire protection. There is no prototype, measured ignition efficacy, vendor quote or comparative field outcome. The relevant alternative is a locally approved, maintained ember-resistant soffit vent, not an open hole.

## Functional unit and assumptions

- Model **1,000 eligible vented homes** with a hypothetical 0.20 m² required soffit intake each. At the README's unverified **0.008 m² loaded NFVA per cassette**, 25 units/home are arithmetically necessary if areas add; actual roof geometry, intake/exhaust balance and local code may require a different design or make installation impossible.
- Installed cassette cost assumed **US$60/unit** = README's unquoted **US$32 ex-workshop target** + assumed **US$28/unit installation**. Excludes survey, permitting, soffit conversion, access, taxes, disposal and any extra ventilation retrofit. Thus this model is a *lower-bound partial budget*, not a bid.
- Routine cleaning/parts allowance **US$5/unit-year**, from the README; assume 10-year service without replacement (unproven). No discounting or inflation; comparison requires measured lifecycle cost of a matched approved vent. Double maintenance sensitivity is US$10/unit-year.
- Exposure frequency below is the assumed annual probability of a qualifying ember event at each home; conditional vent-attributable ignition is the assumed probability of a building ignition *through this inlet* given such exposure under the approved-vent baseline. Relative reduction is the cassette's assumed additional effectiveness versus that baseline, **not** a lab ember-capture percentage. All three probabilities are hypothetical, not empirical estimates. Other ignition paths are excluded.
- For a dollar illustration only, assign **US$250,000 per avoided structure ignition** (not a market loss estimate); do not interpret as insurer savings or lives saved. No probability of harm from blocked ventilation is monetized, and any such harm lowers net value.

## Arithmetic and sensitivity

Capital = 1,000 homes × 25 units × US$60 = **US$1,500,000**. Ten-year routine service = 1,000 × 25 × US$5 × 10 = **US$1,250,000**. Partial 10-year cost = **US$2,750,000**, or US$2,750/home. Double the annual service allowance and total becomes **US$4,000,000** before omitted costs.

Expected additional ignitions averted (not houses demonstrably saved) = homes × years × annual qualifying-exposure probability × conditional vent-attributable ignition probability × *incremental* relative reduction versus approved vent. This deliberately does not treat ember exclusion as a direct fire-loss rate.

| Scenario (assumptions only) | Annual exposure | Baseline conditional vent ignition | Incremental relative reduction | Expected additional ignitions averted, 10 y | Hypothetical loss avoided at US$250k/event |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Low | 1% | 2% | 10% | 0.20 | US$50,000 |
| Middle | 3% | 5% | 25% | 3.75 | US$937,500 |
| High | 5% | 10% | 40% | 20.00 | US$5,000,000 |

Break-even on this deliberately incomplete cost model would need **11 additional ignitions averted** across 1,000 homes in 10 years at US$250,000/event; neither the probability assumptions nor that loss value is validated. A zero or negative incremental efficacy versus approved vents yields no fire benefit and could increase risk. Omitted conversion, survey, replacement and moisture-remediation costs raise the threshold; emissions and materials from 25 metal assemblies/home also require a measured lifecycle comparison. These numbers cannot justify deployment without paired fire/air/water trials, professional ventilation design and permitted field evidence.
