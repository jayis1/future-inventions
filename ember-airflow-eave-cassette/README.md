# Ember-Airflow Eave Cassette

**Author: jayis1** · **Category: Disaster Resilience & Healthy Housing / Wildfire Retrofit** · **Status: research concept (TRL 2; untested)**

## Problem

Wind-driven embers can enter attic vents and ignite concealed combustibles. IBHS experiments found that vent location, orientation, screen size and baffles affect ember entry; a soffited eave with fine metal mesh performed better than an exposed open-eave inlet in the tested configurations.[1][2] Simply covering an existing vent can also compromise attic airflow: ventilation and moisture control must be evaluated for the entire roof, not inferred from an intact screen.[3] Exposed housing communities need an affordable retrofit whose protection and airflow can both be inspected over years of dust, rain and maintenance.

## Solution

The **Ember-Airflow Eave Cassette** is a proposed, removable *soffit-inlet* module for eligible **vented** attics with an already boxed, noncombustible eave. A 304 stainless-steel frame carries a corrosion-resistant 1/16-inch (about 1.6 mm) aperture woven-metal outer screen, a shallow formed-steel two-turn baffle with downward-draining trough, and a captive, tool-openable service door. A second fixed stainless barrier with apertures no larger than 1/8 inch stays attached to the soffit while the outer cassette is withdrawn, so cleaning never leaves a bare attic opening; it is not assumed to pass ember/flame qualification on its own. A snap-in, no-power differential-pressure test port across a representative cassette allows a qualified inspector to compare clean and dirty flow resistance at the *same imposed test flow*. A high-resistance marker calls for cleaning; it is not a fire-safety or attic-moisture certificate. The outer face and drain are visible from below; fasteners and keyed geometry discourage incomplete reseating.

The module does not substitute for converting combustible/open eaves to a compliant soffit, maintaining a fire-resistant roof, keeping the near-home zone clear, or replacing unsafe existing ventilation. A building professional must verify that the whole attic retains its locally required **net free vent area** (NFVA), inlet/outlet balance and moisture strategy before and after installation.[3] No roof is suitable by default.

## Key Innovation

Metal mesh, baffles and listed ember-resistant vents already exist; IBHS tested such designs years ago.[2] The proposed research delta is a **serviceable dual-barrier retrofit with a measurable loaded-airflow acceptance gate**: a permanently retained inner ember barrier during outer-screen cleaning, plus an accessible drain and test port for *paired* fire-entry, rain and ventilation trials. The study must compare this assembly against **locally approved, maintained wildfire-resistant soffit vents**, not against an unsafe unscreened opening. Novelty beyond this repository, superiority and patentability are not established.

This differs from the repository's [Autonomous Wildfire Interceptor Swarm](../autonomous-wildfire-interceptor-swarm/) (outdoor detection and suppression), [Smoke-Refuge Witness Cassette](../smoke-refuge-witness-cassette/) (indoor recirculating particle filtration during smoke), and [Continuous-Load-Path Roof Tie](../continuous-load-path-roof-tie/) (wind uplift connections). None tests **simultaneous long-term ember exclusion and retained attic intake through an inspectable eave retrofit**.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 design target:** US$32 ex-workshop per 300 × 100 mm cassette at 50,000 units/year: formed stainless screen and retained inner barrier US$12, galvanized or coated steel baffle/frame US$8, captive service hardware and test port US$5, fabrication and QA US$7. Add a planning allowance of US$5 per cassette-year for cleaning, fasteners and replacement parts; neither amount is a vendor quote. Roof survey, noncombustible soffit conversion, installation, access equipment, code review, testing, taxes and disposal are excluded and may dominate total cost. Prefer screw-assembled replaceable metal components with compatible corrosion-protected interfaces between stainless and coated steel, recycled-content steel where qualified, and local scrap-metal recovery; contaminated debris is collected under local rules, not swept into the attic.

## Impact

**All figures below are unmeasured design gates, not achieved protection.** For a 300 × 100 mm cassette tested in a defined mock soffit, target **≥0.010 m² measured clean NFVA per cassette** and **≥0.008 m² after a specified dust/needle loading cycle**, with the baffle and *both* barriers in place. These areas are product-level targets, not a ventilation sizing rule: for an illustrative 0.20 m² required soffit intake, **25 loaded cassettes** would be needed if their effective areas were additive and the installed layout permitted them. A qualified designer must check the actual code, roof geometry, free areas at other vents and moisture balance. At a fixed **0.01 m³/s test flow per cassette**, target **≤25 Pa clean** and **≤45 Pa after loading**; a pressure indication alone cannot prove natural-wind flow, moisture safety or ember exclusion.

In replicated wind-driven-ember and direct-flame tests under an applicable vent protocol such as ASTM E2886/E2886M, target **no sustained ignition of a standardized combustible witness behind the installed vent** and report *all* penetrant embers, including those that self-extinguish. Compare the same units before and after dust/needle loading, 500 cleaning/reseating cycles, corrosion exposure, icing/thawing and wind-driven-rain testing; target no more attic-side rain collection than a compliant matched comparator. A visible reseating fault or displaced inner barrier means **out of service**, not permission to run an unprotected opening. If loading makes the target NFVA unattainable or cleaning defeats the retained barrier, reject the design. No prototype, certification, fire-loss reduction or health benefit has been demonstrated.

Over 10–20 years, bench fire/airflow/rain trials, climate-specific roof moisture simulations, third-party listing and a permitted paired retrofit study could determine whether the maintenance design is worthwhile. A 2050 goal is locally repairable hardening for exposed homes if the assembly passes tests and local code review; the number of modules sold is not evidence of fewer fires. Household ignition can also occur through roofs, windows, decks and adjacent fuels, so this is only one layer of resilience.

## Failure boundaries and validation

| Failure | Consequence and detection | Safe response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dust, insect nests, paint or ice blocks mesh | NFVA/flow falls; visual inspection and same-flow pressure test flag service, but localized blockage can escape one port | Inspect every inlet, clear under a service plan; if airflow cannot be restored, redesign ventilation rather than remove barriers |
| Corrosion, broken fastening or wrong-size replacement | Embers or rain may bypass; inspect seam, latch and metal condition | Mark cassette out of service; replace with qualified part, verify fit and barrier continuity |
| Outer door left open during cleaning | Reduced ember protection even though inner screen remains | Captive door and high-contrast seating marks; close and inspect before returning to service; never leave service access open |
| Inner barrier missing or damaged | No reliable ember exclusion while outer cartridge is removed; an ordinary pressure reading may still look normal | Stop service and restore both barriers; reject any design whose failure cannot be noticed at inspection |
| Rain backflow or drainage blockage | Wet sheathing and mold risk; inspect trough and attic after storms | Clear drain and repair roof as needed; do not claim water-tightness from a dry lab test |

Scope is passive, offline and limited to soffit intakes of eligible vented attics; no cameras, radios, automated closures or claims of life-safety certification. An installation needs a licensed/qualified building practitioner where required, an approved vent/fire test and the authority having jurisdiction's acceptance. IBHS notes that finer screens need more cleaning and that ember-resistant vent design matters beyond aperture alone.[2]
