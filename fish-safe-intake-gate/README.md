# Fish-Safe Intake Gate

**Author: jayis1**

**Category:** Freshwater Restoration & Smallholder Irrigation / Fish-Safe Water Diversion
**Status:** Research concept (TRL 2); not an approved fish screen or irrigation structure.

## Problem

Small irrigation diversions can carry juvenile fish and other aquatic animals into ditches or pumps. A screen can exclude animals larger than its openings, but an excessively fast approach flow can pin fish against it; accumulated leaves and sediment can also starve the irrigator of water. Species, life stages, local flow, and site-specific permits determine whether a particular screen protects fish.[1] This affects river ecosystems and the people relying on irrigated food and inland fisheries; no global population or mortality reduction is assumed.

## Solution

A modular, **in-stream, side-looking intake** serves an existing 10 L/s gravity-fed irrigation branch only where a qualified site survey finds a stable downstream sweeping current. Water passes through replaceable, wedge-wire 316L stainless screen panels into a low-head manifold and then a mechanically limited outlet. Fish and floating debris remain in the river and move past the screen; no fish-return ditch is needed for an in-stream installation.[1] A small, guarded brush is driven by the available water head through a serviceable impeller when debris increases resistance; insufficient head leaves it stationary. The brush is isolated from fish by a protective standoff. A float-driven outlet limiter reduces demand at low water levels; a differential-head trip shuts the outlet when fouling exceeds a bench-qualified threshold or the brush stops. An independent staff gauge shows upstream level, and a pressure-tap pair shows screen head loss. Neither head difference nor brush motion alone measures the open screen area; a prototype must map worst-case local velocity under partially blocked panels before a safe trip setting can be specified. A manual shutoff and a removable debris basket allow inspection without opening an unscreened water path. No battery, radio, camera, or remote control is required.

An unpowered spring-and-float linkage limits withdrawal as the river level falls; a mechanical latch prevents a failed or removed screen from leaving the outlet open. This is not a universal bolt-on: insufficient sweeping flow, flood scour, ice, fish eggs or species smaller than the specified aperture require another site-specific design, not a bypass around the screen. An irrigator retains the option to close the branch and use an alternative lawful water supply; the device must not restrict river passage or create an unsafe obstruction.

## Key Innovation

The research hypothesis is a **site-qualified, self-cleaning screen whose withdrawal limit follows a calibrated safe envelope for water level and head loss**. Bench mapping must establish that the outlet does not demand more water than the exposed screen face can pass at the approved *local* approach velocity; when a measured condition leaves that verified envelope, it shuts rather than routing an unsafe unscreened bypass. Unobserved patchy fouling could still defeat a simple head-loss trip and must be included in fault testing. Wedge-wire screens, brushes, floats and intake shutoffs already exist. The proposed advance is the testable low-head linkage and independently observable failure state in a repairable small-diversion module, not a new screening principle or a claim of patent novelty. Unlike the catalog's [Paddy Water-Table Gate](../paddy-water-table-gate/), which schedules refill of rice plots, this device aims to prevent fish entrainment at the *river intake*. Unlike the [Coastal Lens Recharge Gate](../coastal-lens-recharge-gate/), it neither recharges an aquifer nor manages salinity.

**Unverified design envelope:** for 10 L/s diverted flow, aim for local normal approach velocity at most **0.05 m/s**, with at least **0.20 m² minimum effective wetted screen area** (`0.010 m³/s ÷ 0.05 m/s`; screen-frame blockage and fouling require additional gross area). Target a parallel sweeping velocity of at least 0.3 m/s at the *surveyed* operating stages and no more than 0.10 m added head loss at 10 L/s immediately after cleaning. These are conservative research targets, **not** a claim of compliance: the competent fishery authority must specify the aperture, velocity, opening geometry, bypass need, and performance criteria for local species and life stages. The U.S. Bureau of Reclamation describes approach/sweep velocity, wetted-area sizing and site-dependent cleaning; its salmonid example is not universal.[1]

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 planning goal, 10,000 modules/year:** US$250 ex-workshop per 10 L/s module: screen panels and frame US$85, molded recycled-HDPE manifold and guarded brush US$65, linkage/gate/springs US$55, assembly and leak/flow testing US$45. Excludes civil works, qualified ecological and hydraulic survey, installation, permits, annual inspection, replacement screens and transport. Budget a further **unverified US$25/year** for local brush/seal service and allow for periodic panel replacement. This estimate is not a vendor quote or a demonstration of economic viability; affordability depends especially on channel works and water rights.

## Impact

A first paired pilot would compare ten eligible diversions with maintained, regulator-accepted fish screens under the same flows, species and seasonal debris load. Report fish entrainment and impingement per unit volume, screen-face velocity maps, delivered irrigation volume, shutoff events and maintenance hours; do not infer benefit merely from water flowing through a screen. If the mechanism is safe, 10,000 deployed modules could provide at most **100 m³/s of nominal screened diversion capacity** at simultaneous rated flow, not a prediction of actual withdrawal or fish saved. Baseline entrainment, ecological response and food-security effects remain unknown. Lower-cost repairable parts could extend safe screening to small water users, conditional on water-rights and fishery approvals.

## Safety, validation and limits

- **Fish protection:** a local fisheries specialist selects aperture and velocity limits for the weakest relevant life stage; test live-fish passage and contact behavior ethically under approval, including low stage and debris loading. Do not assume salmonid guidelines protect eggs or every species. Screen orientation, vibration, brush contact and bank erosion can harm fish despite low average velocity.[1]
- **Hydraulics:** map *local*, not only area-average, approach velocity near screen edges at maximum withdrawal; independently measure flow and head loss. Block portions of the screen deliberately and confirm the gate closes before the approved velocity is exceeded. Test low river levels, sudden flood debris, sediment wedging, and high-flow scour. A clogged device fails closed, not open to unscreened diversion.
- **Mechanical and human factors:** test jammed brush, broken spring, stuck float, misassembled screen, corroded parts and removal during service. The manual close valve and visible latch should not depend on electricity. A shut gate is an irrigation interruption: provide an operational contingency without illegal or fish-harming bypass. Lock out the branch during maintenance and use guarded moving parts.
- **Lifecycle:** replace worn brush and screen without discarding the HDPE manifold; collect removed debris away from the channel, return compatible metal parts to recycling, and check for polymer shedding. Avoid in-stream installation where protected habitat, sediment transport or flood conveyance would be damaged. No claim of zero environmental footprint or guaranteed fish survival.
- **Path to use:** bench hydraulic mapping and fault injection, then a permitted single-site trial against an approved screen, then multi-season paired trials and local certification within 10–20 years. Abandon or redesign if conventional screens do as well with less ecological or maintenance cost.
