# Flood-Isolating DC Spur — Roadmap

**Author: jayis1** · Research concept, not approved electrical equipment. Each gate is contingent on qualified independent review.

## Concept

Specify a single-source, isolated 24 V DC, ≤4 A nonessential spur and a two-spur elevated module. Map water arrival paths, source fault current, cable coordination, sensor coverage, contact welding, downstream capacitance and reset authority. Exit only when an independent electrical engineer agrees on a testable safety case and a conventional elevated/fused baseline.

## Prototype

Build instrumented dry-module and replaceable-cartridge samples with selected DC-rated two-pole disconnects and coordinated branch protection. Inject fresh/salt/dirty water, open probes, power loss, stuck drivers, reverse feeds and welded contacts; measure actual pole opening, residual voltage and false trips against the README's unverified targets. Exit only with repeatable results, documented failures and independent review of interruption and insulation tests; no occupied-site trial yet.

## Pilot

After applicable approvals, environmental/ingress qualification and site-specific engineering, compare 50 sites with matched conventional isolated DC branches, only on nonessential loads. Inspect before restart and log exposures, missed detections, nuisance trips, service labor and costs without treating zero observed injuries as proof of safety. Exit only if independently audited evidence supports a net benefit and safe maintenance capability.

## Scale

If the pilot clears its gates, source rated components, qualify suppliers and assembly tests, and publish a service manual, traceable failure reports and independent conformity evidence for each jurisdiction. Validate actual installed and lifecycle cost against simple elevation; withdraw or redesign variants that fail wet-fault or serviceability checks.

## Ubiquity

By 2050, offer the design as an optional inspectable boundary for nonessential isolated-DC spurs only where it outperforms compliant elevated wiring. Continued use requires field surveillance, replaceable parts, recycling and periodic independent safety review; widespread adoption is an aspiration, not an engineering or market forecast.
