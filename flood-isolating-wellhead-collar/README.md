# Flood-Isolating Wellhead Collar

**Author: jayis1**
**Status: TRL 2 concept; not a certified well seal or a declaration that flood-exposed water is potable.**

## Problem

About one in eight US residents obtains drinking water from a private well; this is a large, dispersed population whose water safety depends on the well and its maintenance.[2] Floods can damage private-well piping and electrics and introduce contamination. CDC says not to drink from a possibly contaminated well after extensive flooding until it is assessed, disinfected as appropriate, and tested; fuel or chemical contamination cannot be made safe merely by boiling or chlorination.[1]

A flood can enter through a low vent or a damaged well cap, but also through the casing, annulus or submerged plumbing. A device at the wellhead can address only the first set of paths. Existing sanitary well caps and elevated vents are the baseline, not inventions to rename: EPA guidance already calls for sanitary seals and a casing vent above flood level.[3]

## Solution

A licensed well contractor fits a **removable, size-specific collar** around an intact drilled-well casing at a site with a surveyed flood design elevation. A compression-limited **EPDM gasket** seals the split **glass-filled polypropylene** sleeve against the casing without drilling it. A replaceable **316 stainless-steel** clamping band holds the sleeve; separately gasketed electrical and water-line bulkheads route existing services without squeezing their insulation or tubing. A rigid, screened gooseneck vent terminates above the locally approved flood level and remains open during normal pumping. It must meet local well-code venting requirements; a float shutoff is deliberately *not* used as the primary vent, since a blocked vent can impair pump operation.

An **independent splash-and-submergence witness** is a captive mechanical indicator exposed at the lowest vulnerable joint. It changes state on contact with floodwater and stays visibly tripped until a contractor resets it after inspection. A dry-contact switch may inhibit pump power through a qualified electrical control when that witness trips, but the mechanical flag works without power. Neither a dry flag nor an intact collar proves potable water. This is a retrofit proposal for approved casing geometries, not a way to make a below-grade well pit safe.

## Key Innovation

The proposed contribution is the **serviceable package and verification workflow**, not a new waterproof gasket: a replaceable multi-penetration wellhead boundary paired with an *independent* exposure witness and a vent routed above the site-specific flood level. The witness forces a post-flood inspection decision even if the outside looks dry. Unlike `passive-flood-door-sill`, which limits flood pressure across an approved building doorway, this protects a well's cap penetrations and never deliberately admits water to equalize pressure. Unlike `electroferrate-arsenic-remediation-network`, it neither removes arsenic nor treats water. Existing watertight well caps and raised vents may already outperform this concept at many sites; comparison trials must establish whether the modular retrofit has any added value.[3]

**Unverified engineering targets:** qualify intact 100–150 mm outside-diameter casing interfaces; test to **0.8 m static freshwater head for 60 minutes** with zero *visually detectable* ingress at the protected cap penetrations (not a microbiological guarantee); retain the seal after **100 wet/dry cycles** and a separate silt/debris exposure; and require an **annual pre-flood-season inspection**. Every installation still requires a local site assessment and independent well-code approval. No flood-speed, scour, wave-impact, or chemical-spill rating is claimed.

## Target Cost

**Unvalidated 2035 ex-factory target at 100,000 units/year: US$75** per collar kit: US$20 molded sleeve/vent, US$12 EPDM seals, US$14 stainless band and bulkheads, US$9 captive witness/switch interface, US$20 assembly and pressure/leak checks. Installation, well survey, elevated vent support, electrical modifications, taxes and any repair to casing/annulus are additional and may dominate the bill. Target replaceable seal/witness parts: US$12 per scheduled service. A compliant existing cap and raised vent should remain in service when cheaper or safer; no savings versus existing art are established.

## Impact

A bounded pilot would compare **100 eligible wells** fitted by qualified contractors against matched, code-compliant cap-and-raised-vent controls. Log water ingress at each penetration under the same hydraulic exposure, indicator false trips, vent pressure drop during pump duty, service time, and *separate* microbiological/chemical water tests. At a hypothetical 100,000 installations, at most 100,000 wellheads would gain a monitored boundary; the number of contamination events prevented cannot be inferred without trials. The goal is fewer preventable cap-path incursions, not an assertion that flood-affected households can keep drinking untreated well water.

**Failure and safety:** Silt can obstruct the vent; corrosion or gasket compression set can open a seam; a failed witness can miss submergence; floodwater can bypass an intact collar through a cracked casing or annular seal. Stop pumping if the vent blocks or the witness trips; a contractor must inspect the whole well. After any flood that may have affected a well, use safe alternative water and follow local health guidance for inspection, disinfection and laboratory testing regardless of the indicator.[1] A qualified electrician assesses wet controls before energizing. Do not install in a pit or on damaged casing, and do not work on energized or flooded well equipment. Replace worn elastomers, recycle stainless parts where possible, and route polymer parts to an appropriate local stream. The principal 10–20-year development gate is independently demonstrating that varied field installations remain sealed *without compromising ventilation* after weathering and imperfect maintenance; abandon the retrofit where a conventional elevated wellhead is more reliable. It has no telemetry, camera, personal-data collection, remote switching, military, surveillance or coercive function.

## How It Works

1. **Qualify the site.** A licensed contractor checks an intact 100–150 mm outside-diameter casing, its cap, annular grout, service exits and surveyed flood elevation. A compliant elevated cap and vent remain the first comparator; this retrofit is rejected if the casing or annulus is defective.
2. **Install the dry-state boundary.** The split glass-filled polypropylene sleeve closes around the casing. Compression stops limit deformation of its replaceable EPDM gasket; a 316 stainless band retains it. Individually sized, strain-relieved glands seal each water line and pump cable without pinching insulation. The screened, downward-facing vent stays open above the approved flood elevation during pumping.
3. **Encounter floodwater.** Rising water loads the cap seam and glands; the sleeve and gaskets oppose entry at those paths. There is no buoyant valve or motorized flood closure to wait for: the cap-path seal is continuously engaged. At the proposed 0.8 m head, freshwater hydrostatic pressure is approximately 7.8 kPa (using density 1,000 kg/m³ and gravity 9.81 m/s²), before dynamic debris or wave loads; this is a calculation, not a rating.
4. **Record exposure, then recover.** A separate mechanical witness at the vulnerable joint latches when wetted, including during a power outage. Its optional dry contact can request a pump inhibit through approved controls, but a failed contact does not change the mechanical seal. After suspected flooding, stop use, obtain safe alternative water, and arrange inspection and appropriate disinfection/testing; a reset flag is never a potable-water certificate.[1]

## Technical Architecture

| Subsystem | Physical role and interface |
| --- | --- |
| Casing boundary | Two-piece polymer shell, compression-limited EPDM gasket and replaceable stainless band bear on intact casing; no drilling or structural flood protection is claimed. |
| Penetrations | Dedicated strain-relieved cable and water-line bulkheads close bypass paths; incompatible existing connectors disqualify the installation. The casing annulus and buried plumbing remain outside the boundary. |
| Ventilation | Rigid screened gooseneck terminates above the site-approved flood level and stays open during normal pumping. Measure pressure drop at the actual pump's start and running flow; stop pumping if blocked. No submerged shutoff valve may silently create a vacuum. |
| Exposure and control | Independent captive wetting witness latches visibly; optional dry contact reports a trip or requests a code-approved pump inhibit. No network, telemetry or water-quality sensor is implied. |

The physical path is **floodwater → outer shell/cap/glands → protected cap opening**; the vent path is **well air ↔ elevated screened opening**. The separate information path is **wet joint → latched witness → human inspection**, optionally also **dry contact → approved pump control**. Neither path senses contamination arriving through groundwater, cracked casing, defective grout or downstream plumbing.

## Performance Benchmarks

These are **unverified qualification targets**, not field results. Test on matched casings against a code-compliant watertight cap with raised vent; do not assume the collar outperforms that current practice.[3]

| Metric | Proposed target / test condition | Current practice and evidence gap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Static cap-path ingress | No *visible* ingress at cap/glands during 0.8 m freshwater head held 60 min, including after silt exposure and 100 wet/dry cycles; measure collected leakage in mL and report the detection limit. | A compliant cap and elevated vent face the same challenge in the paired test; no comparative measurements yet. A visual pass cannot rule out microbes. |
| Vent performance | No obstruction or unacceptable pressure drop at the actual pump's startup and running air demand, both before and after debris/cycle testing; set a numeric pressure limit from the qualified pump/well design. | Raised screened vents already allow normal ventilation; no demonstrated advantage. |
| Fit and witness | Qualify only 100–150 mm casing OD with verified compression and undamaged service glands; measure false trips and misses under rain, splash and immersion before setting thresholds. | Conventional caps may lack an exposure flag, but a flag alone measures wetting, not ingress. |
| Cost and upkeep | Unvalidated 2035 ex-factory target US$75/kit at 100,000 units/year; US$12 replacement seal/witness parts; annual pre-season inspection. Contractor installation, survey and repairs extra. | Compare a site-specific quote for a compliant cap and raised vent; no installed-cost savings established. |

No measured flood-speed, impact, scour, wave, freeze–thaw or chemical-resistance rating exists. Mechanical leakage, microbial assays and chemical testing require distinct acceptance decisions; independent testing and local sanitary/electrical approval precede any household deployment.

## Deployment Scenarios

- **River-floodplain private wells:** A county health department or well contractor first maps flood elevations and selects intact drilled wells whose existing cap/raised vent arrangement leaves a remediable penetration risk. The contractor installs and pressure-checks the approved fit; the owner checks the witness and vent before flood season. An existing compliant elevated head may be the better purchase.
- **Coastal surge-prone homes:** A local well service firm evaluates surge, debris and salt exposure before considering a collar. Sites beyond the static freshwater test envelope are excluded until separate impact, corrosion and saltwater protocols pass; a tripped witness initiates the same post-flood testing pathway as any flooded well.
- **Small community well program:** A water NGO or local utility can evaluate multiple compatible drilled wells in a controlled, matched trial, budgeting for licensed installation, annual gasket service and independent microbial/chemical samples. Shallow hand-pump or dug wells are not interchangeable with this drilled-well retrofit.

## Risks & Mitigations

| Failure or limitation | Mitigation / decision |
| --- | --- |
| Vent screen clogs or vent outlet is overtopped; pumping draws a vacuum | Site-specific surveyed vent height, pump-duty pressure-drop test, accessible screen inspection and pump stop on blocked vent. Reject sites without a safely supported high vent. |
| Silt, freeze–thaw, UV, corrosion or gasket compression set opens a seam | Separate accelerated-aging and silt/cycle qualification; inspect pre-season and after storms, replace damaged EPDM/glands and re-test. Electrically isolate dissimilar metals where needed; verify the finished assembly's potable-contact suitability rather than assuming constituent materials certify it. |
| Witness false-trip or missed flood; optional electrical inhibit fails | Independently challenge witness under splash/immersion and power loss; ambiguous state means inspection and alternative water, not clearance. A qualified electrician handles all wet controls. |
| Scour, debris impact, damaged casing, bad grout or contaminated aquifer bypasses the cap | Do not claim structural or aquifer protection. Reject unsuitable sites, inspect the whole well and follow health-authority guidance for testing/disinfection and chemical hazards after suspected flooding.[1] |
| Code incompatibility, installation error or unaffordable service | Licensed contractor and local sanitary/electrical approval before trials; compare full installed lifecycle cost with a standard compliant cap and raised vent. Stop the product program if maintenance or reliability is worse. |

**Concept only:** no certification, flood protection rating or drinking-water safety claim. A successful seal test does not replace qualified well-contractor assessment or post-flood water testing.[1]

## Vision for 2050

A plausible **scenario, not a forecast**: floodplain well registries make it routine to identify vulnerable cap penetrations before the next flood, while licensed contractors fit either proven raised conventional heads or qualified retrofit boundaries where surveys justify them. Owners see an unmistakable exposure flag and know whom to call, rather than treating a clean-looking cap as a water-quality test. The collar earns a place only if paired trials establish an incremental benefit without degrading ventilation or post-flood vigilance; otherwise the better 2050 outcome is widespread use of conventional elevated, sanitary wellheads.[3]
