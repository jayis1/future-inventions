# Flood-Safe Egg Sink

**Author: jayis1** · **Status: research concept (TRL 2)** · **Category: Public Health & Universal Basic Services / Mosquito Source Reduction**

## Problem

Dengue puts about half the world's population at risk; WHO estimates 100–400 million infections annually and reports 14.6 million cases in 2024.[1] Container-breeding *Aedes* mosquitoes exploit small water reservoirs around homes. An attractive but neglected egg trap could itself become a breeding site, especially when rain overtops it. Source reduction and proven bite-prevention measures remain essential; a trap cannot substitute for them.

## Solution

The **Flood-Safe Egg Sink** is a manually serviced, chemical-free outdoor oviposition trap for *Aedes*. A dark, UV-stabilized polypropylene body holds 0.75 L of water and a removable cellulose egg-laying strip above the nominal waterline. An inward-facing entry funnel admits gravid females into a captive adult chamber above the egg strip; the funnel tip is shielded by a replaceable sticky liner to obstruct escape by emerging adults (unproven until challenge testing). A 0.4 mm stainless screen isolates the breeding-water chamber below; a second, independently attached 0.4 mm screened overflow drains rainfall through a downward-facing, insect-blocked outlet. A captive float raises a red mechanical flag when water reaches the overflow; a separate red tab becomes visible if either screen or lid is unseated. A tamper-visible lid prevents accidental open-container operation. No electronics, cloud, pesticide, or person-level data.

Eggs and larvae remain inside a sealed collection volume until the operator removes the strip and destroys captured material according to local vector-control guidance. Refill weekly with clean water and replace the egg strip and sticky liner; clean screens and inspect seals weekly and after heavy rain. Reusable housing and replaceable generic mesh/strip allow local repair. **Do not deploy where weekly servicing and safe disposal cannot be guaranteed**: remove, drain and store dry instead. This is a source-reduction research tool, not protection against bites or disease by itself.

## Key Innovation

Ovitraps and adult-emergence mesh barriers already exist.[2] The proposed research question is whether a **redundant, independently sealed overflow route plus an unpowered fault flag and paper service log** can prevent a trap from becoming a mosquito producer during flooding or missed servicing, without reducing egg capture relative to an ordinary autocidal ovitrap. The innovation is failure containment and inspectability, not the discovery of mosquito oviposition or a claim of proven vector control. The existing repository's Acoustic Vector Disruption Array proposes acoustic mating interference; this is a passive, locally serviceable breeding-site containment device, not an emitter or a population-listening network.

## Target Cost

**Unverified design target:** US$18 ex-factory per unit at 100,000/year by 2038 (housing/lid US$6, two stainless screens and supports US$4, float/flag US$2, gasket/fasteners US$2, assembly/quality testing US$4); US$0.35 per cellulose-strip/sticky-liner pair weekly and approximately US$2/year for replacement screens/gasket. At 52 pairs/year the consumables target is US$18.20/year, or US$20.20/year including replacement parts, excluding labor, transport, taxes, disposal, deployment planning and health surveillance. Five-year housing life is a target subject to UV and corrosion testing. Cost per case averted is **unknown** until a controlled field trial measures disease outcomes.

## Impact

**Pilot target, not a demonstrated benefit:** 500 serviced traps in a 12-month, matched-neighborhood study; ≥90% of units remain escape-proof under simulated 100 mm/day rainfall and weekly servicing, with zero adult emergence from intact units under lab challenge. Compare eggs captured and adult emergence against standard mesh ovitraps, plus the same source-reduction education in both arms. If the device performs no better than a simpler trap, discontinue development. Even perfect capture from these units would not control uncollected containers or imported mosquitoes; no reduction in dengue incidence or lives saved is claimed. Public-health authorities must review siting, disposal, non-target capture and field-trial ethics before use.

## How It Works

1. **Prepare:** Seat the EPDM lid gasket and both independent ≤0.4 mm 316-stainless screens in the 1.5 L UV-stabilized polypropylene body. Charge with 0.75 L clean water; fit a fresh cellulose oviposition strip and non-insecticidal sticky liner. Check the red seating tab, float and paper service log before placing the trap out of children's reach.
2. **Attract and contain:** A gravid *Aedes* female approaches through the inward-facing baffled funnel into the adult chamber and may lay eggs on the strip above the normal waterline. The proposed liner obstructs the reverse route, while the chamber screen isolates water and developing insects. Neither the funnel nor liner is an established escape barrier: whole-life challenges must show zero adult escape before field use. Mesh alone is not claimed to retain eggs.
3. **Rain and service:** Gravity carries excess rain through the separately screened, downward-facing overflow. With an assumed unobstructed 100 cm² catchment, 100 mm of rain adds 1 L—more than the nominal charge; this is an overflow-design input, not a demonstrated capacity. A raised float or visible red seating tab means withdraw the unit. Each week, and after heavy rain, inspect for clogging and damage, replace strip and liner, and dispose of captured material and water under the local vector-control protocol using the insect-proof service sleeve. Never release live contents while cleaning.
4. **Flood failure:** If the entrance or outlet is submerged, a screen unseats, the outlet blocks, or servicing lapses, remove the unit rather than trusting the flag. Drain safely and store dry pending repair. A fault indicator is not evidence that an unattended trap is safe.

## Technical Architecture

| Assembly | Function and boundary |
| --- | --- |
| Housing and lid | 1.5 L polypropylene shell, EPDM gasket and tamper-visible lid; 0.75 L nominal water charge; no electronics or grid power. |
| Entry and capture | Inward baffled funnel, cellulose egg strip and replaceable sticky liner; adult retention is a test hypothesis, not a certified property. |
| Water and insect paths | Internal ≤0.4 mm mesh separates adult chamber from potentially contaminated water; independently fitted ≤0.4 mm overflow screen covers the gravity drain. Water exits only through the screened route in the intended, unsubmerged configuration. |
| Inspection and waste | Captive float/flag indicates high water; red seating tab exposes a displaced lid or screen; operator records visits on paper, withdraws faulted units, and uses a fine-mesh service sleeve to drain. No personal data is collected. |

The two meshes address different escape routes, not a claim of two fully independent barriers on every path: an adult could potentially reverse through the funnel. Prototype tests must challenge that path, lid tolerances, debris loads and aged adhesive. A sealed bucket or routinely serviced conventional screened ovitrap may deliver equal value for less; the extra overflow and fault signals earn their cost only if comparative trials show fewer hazardous failures without materially reducing egg capture.

## Performance Benchmarks

No prototype measurements or disease-outcome data exist. The following are **acceptance targets**, compared with a conventional screened ovitrap under identical enclosure, rain and servicing conditions—not measured improvements over commercial devices. See [SPECIFICATION.md](./SPECIFICATION.md) for the challenge protocol.

| Metric and test condition | Proposed target | Comparator / current evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adult escape, 30 complete units challenged over two full local *Aedes* development cycles, including aged liners and tolerance extremes | Zero escape from intact units; any escape stops field deployment | Measure alongside ordinary screened ovitrap; no comparable result available yet. |
| Sealing and drainage, 30 units at 100 mm/day rain including 50 mm/hour pulse, leaf/silt load and partial blockage | ≥90% remain sealed and draining; every failure visibly flags or is withdrawn before use | Same test on conventional trap; 90% engineering yield does **not** permit 10% adult escape. |
| Egg capture in paired enclosures | Non-inferior capture to conventional screened ovitrap, with no adult escape | Baseline must be measured; trapping eggs alone is not population suppression. |
| Wear and care | 1,000 gasket/float cycles, UV/heat and salt-spray exposure, weekly service plus post-rain inspection | No durability or service-time measurements yet; reject failed seals, mesh, float or liner. |

At 100 cm² catchment, a 50 mm/hour pulse implies 0.5 L/hour inflow before wind, splash or debris effects; the actual wet catchment and flow-through capacity must be measured. The US$18 ex-factory and US$20.20 annual parts/consumables figures above are cost-model targets, not purchase prices; labor remains unpriced.

## Deployment Scenarios

- **Authority-run flood-prone neighborhood pilot:** Place 500 units only where trained staff can visit weekly for 12 months, inspect after storms and safely dispose of captured material. Compare against standard screened traps with identical source-reduction education; track adult escape and service failures, not assumed dengue cases prevented.
- **Small community vector-control route:** If bench gates pass, a 10,000-unit network could be serviced in scheduled rounds with spare mesh, EPDM gaskets and strips. This is a planning scenario, not a deployment claim; route time, waste handling and local authorization determine whether it is affordable.
- **Sites with intermittent access or inundated outlets:** Do not install a water-filled device where weekly visits or dry overflow cannot be maintained. A dry-stored kit, source removal or another authority-approved measure is preferable to a neglected ovitrap that might breed mosquitoes.

## Risks & Mitigations

- **Adult escape through funnel, lid or degraded liner:** Challenge complete, aged assemblies over two development cycles; any escape is a redesign and deployment stop, not an acceptable yield loss. Visibly unseated components require immediate withdrawal.
- **Debris-clogged overflow, overtopping or submerged outlet:** Test 100 mm/day rain and short pulses with leaf/silt load; inspect after storms. Float warning cannot prove flow, and submergence is outside the operating envelope: remove, drain safely and dry-store.
- **Eggs, larvae or contaminated water released during servicing:** Do not assume mesh retains eggs. Train handlers, use the insect-proof service sleeve and locally approved destruction/disposal procedure; never pour untreated contents into another breeding site.
- **Missed visits, exposure to children or animals, adhesive failure:** Keep a paper log and spare parts; a missed scheduled service or damaged liner means withdraw the unit. Shield adhesive, secure siting and audit visit completion rather than treating an unobserved trap as safe.
- **Cost without incremental benefit:** Compare with a simpler serviced screened trap and habitat removal. If attraction falls, failure rates do not improve or labor dominates cost, stop rather than scale; no infection-prevention claim follows from egg counts.

## Vision for 2050

If controlled comparisons demonstrate safe containment, practical weekly servicing and a real advantage over simpler traps, a repairable egg sink could be one optional item in locally managed flood-season vector-control kits. The 2050 vision is an auditable routine—inspect, log, safely empty or withdraw—not an unattended device that ends dengue. Where flooding, staffing or comparative evidence fails the gates, the better future is fewer water-holding containers, not more traps.
