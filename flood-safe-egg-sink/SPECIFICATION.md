# Flood-Safe Egg Sink — Research Specification

**Author: jayis1** · All figures below are design inputs or test targets, not field measurements.

## Architecture and physical limits

- UV-stabilized polypropylene 1.5 L molded body, 0.75 L nominal water charge; replaceable EPDM lid gasket; two independent 316 stainless woven meshes with ≤0.4 mm clear aperture at chamber separation and overflow outlet; captive polypropylene float and red mechanical indicator; inward-facing baffled funnel and replaceable non-insecticidal sticky liner; removable cellulose oviposition strip.
- Funnel and liner must retain gravid adults while allowing approach to the strip; captured eggs and any larvae must remain behind the first screen. The screen is not presumed to exclude eggs: eggs are retained by the strip and water below is treated as contaminated. Float indicator is a service signal, not proof of disease protection. Any red indication or missing part means withdraw the unit, drain through an insect-proof fine-mesh service sleeve and store dry pending repair.
- For a 100 cm² unobstructed catchment and 100 mm rain event, incoming rain is 1.0 L (0.01 m² × 0.1 m × 1,000 L/m³); it exceeds the 0.75 L nominal charge. The overflow must therefore safely convey at least 1 L per such event and be tested at high short-duration rainfall rates. Do not place it where flooding submerges the entrance or outlet; submerged operation is a **fail** and requires removal. The catchment and entrained debris must be measured in prototypes, not assumed.
- Gravity powers overflow; no battery or network is needed. Screens may clog with leaves, biofilm or mud. A raised float warns of rising water but cannot guarantee a clear outlet; inspect after rain and stop use if blocked. The sticky liner may dry or foul, and the entry funnel may let adults escape: neither is assumed safe without full-life testing. Keep away from children and animals; avoid exposed adhesives.

## Bench acceptance tests before any outdoor pilot

1. Challenge 30 complete units with locally relevant *Aedes* eggs/larvae, screen gaps, lid tolerance extremes and aged sticky liners over two full development cycles. **Target: zero adult escape from intact units**; any escape halts field deployment pending redesign.
2. Challenge 30 units with controlled 100 mm/day simulated rainfall, including a 50 mm/hour pulse, with standardized leaf and silt loading and with partial overflow obstruction. **Target: ≥90% remain sealed and draining; 100% of failed units visibly indicate failure or are removed before deployment.** The 90% engineering yield is not an acceptable adult-escape rate.
3. Compare egg capture and adult production in paired enclosures against an ordinary screened ovitrap and an empty-control container; record non-target capture. Target non-inferior egg capture with no escape, but abandon if the redundant screens suppress attraction materially.
4. Cycle gaskets and float 1,000 times; expose housing to UV and heat aging, screens to salt spray, and liners to humidity; retest seal and flag visibility. Reject brittle, corroded or adhesive-failed units. Test safe cleaning and handling of potentially infectious water under local public-health procedures.

## Field gate and open questions

A vector-control authority must approve siting, maintenance and disposal protocols. In a matched-neighborhood pilot (500 serviced units, 12 months), measure scheduled visits, failures per trap-month, oviposition/escape versus standard traps, nearby container habitats and environmental non-target effects; both arms receive identical source-reduction education. Do not infer dengue cases averted from egg counts. A later cluster-randomized study would be required to estimate health outcomes. Unknowns include attraction when other containers abound, adhesive durability, servicing cost, rainfall under blockage, and whether a simple conventional trap plus reliable maintenance is equally effective. If so, the simpler option wins.
