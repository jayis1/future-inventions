# Fluoride Exchange Water Kiosk

**Author: jayis1** · Concept (TRL 2) · 2026-10-28

A community-owned drinking-and-cooking-water kiosk whose fluoride-removal cartridges are exchanged before exhaustion, with regeneration and contaminated rinse confined to a trained regional service hub. This is a research proposal, not a certified drinking-water system.

## Problem

A global groundwater model estimates approximately 180 million people may be exposed to fluoride above the WHO drinking-water guideline of 1.5 mg/L; this is a modeled population at risk, not a count of diagnosed fluorosis.[1][2] Long-term excess intake can cause dental and skeletal fluorosis; local limits may need to be lower where water or food intake is high.[2] Wells that look clear can therefore remain unsuitable for drinking and cooking. A filter that silently breaks through, or whose spent chemicals are dumped beside the well, is not a durable solution.

## Solution

A staffed kiosk for a characterized groundwater source sends water through a particle prefilter and two **12 kg granular activated-alumina beds in lead/lag series**, then through a protected product tank and dispensing tap. Alumina surface hydroxyl groups exchange with fluoride at suitable pH; activated alumina and its caustic regeneration are established technologies, not discoveries claimed here.[2][3] Food-contact HDPE or stainless wetted parts, replaceable seals, a mechanical flow meter, locked sample ports and a default-closed outlet make each bed inspectable and exchangeable without opening it at the kiosk.

**Proposed operating envelope, not demonstrated performance:** 100 L/day of drinking/cooking water from a source initially containing 5 mg/L fluoride, with a site-specific laboratory-approved target of **≤1.0 mg/L** at the tap (providing margin below 1.5 mg/L). Treat a maximum of **2,000 L or 20 operating days per lead-bed exchange**, whichever occurs first, *only if* a local pilot verifies this interval. The lag bed is a guard, not a license to use exhausted lead media indefinitely. A service worker swaps sealed lead cartridges, moves the verified lag cartridge to lead position only after inspection, and installs a qualified replacement as lag. No acid or caustic is stored at the kiosk.

The default-closed tap requires a valid power/lock state and a recent passing test; loss of power, overdue sampling, leaking seals, out-of-range pH, excess flow, elevated fluoride, or missing cartridge identification diverts all output to a clearly marked **non-potable** container. A manual hand pump may feed the beds, but cannot bypass the outlet lock. If electricity is unavailable, the kiosk shuts rather than promising untested water.

## Key Innovation

The proposed advance is a **verified exchange-and-custody loop**, not a new adsorbent: paired beds with sampleable intermediate and final ports, a volume-and-calendar interlock, independent calibrated fluoride tests, and sealed return of every exhausted bed to a centralized regeneration hub. Unlike the existing arsenic electroferrate network (arsenic oxidation and iron sorption), this targets dissolved fluoride by alumina surface exchange and explicitly prohibits village-scale chemical regeneration. Existing fluoride and activated-alumina systems provide prior art; the unproven hypothesis is that this architecture will deliver reliably safer water at an affordable *fully serviced* price.

For a starting mass balance, 5 to 1 mg/L at 100 L/day removes **400 mg F/day**; 20 days removes **8 g**. An assumed **1 mg F/g alumina working capacity** makes a 12 kg lead bed nominally capable of 12 g, so an 8 g exchange limit uses two-thirds of that *assumed* capacity. This is not a guaranteed breakthrough time: real run length depends on site water, contact time and competing ions. At 100 L/day the 12 kg bed is roughly 16 L if bulk density is *assumed* 0.75 kg/L; 20 days is about 125 bed volumes, deliberately conservative relative to the several-hundred-to-few-thousand bed-volume range reported for some EPA installations.[3] The pilot must reject these assumptions if lead or lag samples break through earlier.

## Target Cost

**Planning scenario in 2038 USD at 10,000 kiosks/year, not a supplier quote:** US$700 installed per kiosk including first two cartridges, tap lock and basic sampling ports; US$20 per 20-day sealed-cartridge exchange including hub regeneration, transport, safe residual handling and new-media loss; US$120/year for field reagents and independent laboratory checks. At 100 L/day for 365 days (36.5 m³/year), five-year straight-line capital allocation is US$140/year; 18.25 annual exchanges cost US$365/year, giving **US$625/year or ~US$17.1/m³ (1.71 US cents/L)** before operator wages, electricity, finance, source pretreatment, source-water surveys and taxes. A staff or transport shortfall could make the true delivered price substantially higher; alternatives such as a safe lower-fluoride source or professionally maintained reverse osmosis must be compared locally.

## Impact

One kiosk at full modeled utilization could dispense **36,500 L/year**, nominally enough for 50 people at 2 L/person/day for drinking alone; cooking water, outages and differing needs reduce that reach. At 10,000 qualified kiosks the *upper-bound capacity* is 365 million L/year, not an estimate of disease prevented. Community cooperatives could own the stations and receive independent quality results without transmitting personal consumption records. Locally made housings and repairable valves reduce equipment dependency; alumina, washwater and fluoride-rich residues still require audited collection. A 10–20-year development path should prioritize long-lived service institutions over optimistic material claims.

## Safety and failure modes

- **Water chemistry:** Determine pH, alkalinity, silica, phosphate, sulfate, arsenic, other site-specific contaminants and microbiological quality before site selection. pH strongly affects alumina fluoride uptake; bicarbonate and phosphate can reduce effective capacity; sulfate behavior varies with chemistry and acid pretreatment.[3] Do not lower pH at the kiosk without a separately validated neutralization, leached-aluminum and operator-safety plan. Sites outside a validated envelope need a different treatment train.
- **Verification:** A trained worker collects influent, between-bed and tap samples at commissioning and at least twice weekly during pilots, plus immediately after exchange; a calibrated fluoride ion-selective electrode or validated colorimetric method with blanks, standards and independent laboratory split samples is required. Proposed release criterion ≤1.0 mg/L at the tap; intermediate fluoride ≥1.0 mg/L triggers immediate lead exchange and investigation. The final tap stays closed until passing confirmation; continuous fluoride sensing is **not** assumed. Periodic microbiological tests and any needed disinfection are separate requirements: alumina does not make contaminated water microbiologically safe.
- **Regeneration:** At the controlled hub, trained staff can evaluate caustic desorption, acid reconditioning and thorough rinsing under ventilation, PPE and secondary containment. The EPA manual describes approximately 1% NaOH regeneration and acid neutralization; neither is a village procedure.[3] Measure fluoride, pH, aluminum and other leachates before reuse. Collect spent caustic/acid and fluoride-rich rinse in labeled tanks; recover or immobilize contaminants only through a permitted process with measured leachability. No drain, soil or cookware disposal. If a licensed hub cannot manage the waste and certify cartridges, stop service rather than discard it locally.
- **Other failures:** Bed channeling, seal leaks, exhausted guard media and false-negative field tests require pressure/flow and seal checks, duplicate testing and independent audits. If an outlet lock can be bypassed, a source is microbiologically unsafe without validated treatment, or the community cannot obtain safe backup water during shutdown, the design fails its deployment gate.

## Evidence and uncertainties

WHO publishes the 1.5 mg/L guideline and reports activated alumina can achieve 1 mg/L under appropriate conditions.[2] EPA documents established adsorption and regeneration, but also variable working capacity and hazardous acid/caustic handling.[3] The population estimate is a geospatial model, not a clinical trial.[1] The specific 1 mg/g capacity, 20-day exchange interval, US$20 exchange fee and outlet interlock have **not** been validated for this kiosk. No claim of patent novelty or potable-water certification is made.

## Validation milestones and stop criteria

1. **Years 1–3:** Bench column tests on at least three representative local waters at maximum expected fluoride and seasonal water chemistry; measure breakthrough curves at lead and lag outlets, aluminum release, pH and microbial counts. Stop if a certified safe source or simpler proven treatment is cheaper or if 125 bed volumes cannot be safely attained with margin.
2. **Years 4–8:** Independently audited, blinded 12-month paired community pilot against an existing maintained treatment; record every test failure, shutdown, missed service, delivered cost and waste mass. Stop if the tap ever dispenses out-of-spec water after a known failed test or if spent regenerant lacks a lawful disposition.
3. **Years 9–15:** Seek local drinking-water approval, verify regeneration cycle life and hub effluent under permit, train paid regional service teams and publish water-quality data without personal usage logs. Scale only when independently reproduced quality, reliable backup supply and total cost meet local acceptance criteria.
