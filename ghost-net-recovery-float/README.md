# Ghost-Net Recovery Float

**Author: jayis1. Status: unvalidated research concept; not approved fishing gear.**

## Problem

Lost gillnets can continue entangling animals and create persistent plastic litter. FAO identifies gillnets and other entangling gear as particular ghost-fishing concerns, but notes that global loss-volume estimates have limited empirical support.[1] Small-scale fishers may lose an anchored net when a surface marker is cut away or a storm displaces its anchor. A buried or submerged net is then hard to find and expensive to retrieve. The intended users are licensed nearshore fishers working fixed, shallow-water nets, not industrial drifting nets.

## Solution

A serviceable **secondary recovery float** rides on a net's headrope beside its existing legally required surface markers. The float sits inside a reusable polypropylene cage, tethered to the headrope by a separately rated retrieval line on a contained spool. A fisher arms a spring-wound mechanical timer before setting the net. If the net is not recovered or deliberately reset within a site-approved interval (initial trial: 72 hours), the timer opens the cage and an encapsulated closed-cell cork/PE composite float rises while paying out line. A large engraved physical ownership/permit mark and retroreflective patch make it visible without GPS, radio, batteries or tracking of people. The float does **not** lift the net; retrieval requires a qualified crew and an approved vessel procedure. Set depth is limited by the actual spool and buoyancy rating (initial design: ≤20 m); it cannot help if the headrope or tether breaks, if the float is trapped, or if the net drifts beyond the search area.

## Key Innovation

Surface buoys, physical gear marks, acoustic locators and timed fishing-gear releases already exist.[1][2] The proposed distinction is a **timer-released, normally submerged backup surface marker mechanically coupled to a retained retrieval line**, with a manual reset at each legitimate haul. It aims to expose a lost net's position even when its ordinary surface line is severed, without demanding a powered receiver or broadcasting location. It is not a substitute for ordinary marking, reporting or timely removal; a clock alone cannot distinguish lost gear from fishing gear. Pilot targets are ≥90% marker surfacing within 15 minutes of the set interval in a 0–20 m test range, ≥95% successful line payout without tangles over 100 wet cycles, and zero net lift or line break during a 0.5 kN proof pull. These are test goals, not achieved results. Net-specific anchoring, buoy size and retrieval loads must be independently engineered; 0.5 kN is a test load, not permission to pull a snagged net.

## Target Cost

**Unvalidated 2038 ex-factory target:** US$45 per module at 100,000/year: molded cage and sealed float US$10, 25 m replaceable retrieval-line spool US$8, corrosion-resistant spring/timer/latch US$15, marking and reflector US$2, assembly and pressure/cycle test US$10. Allow approximately US$10/year for inspection, line replacement and refurbishment, excluding crew, vessel, licensing, gear adaptation, search, installation and end-of-life collection. Supplier quotes and sea trials could invalidate this estimate; community cooperatives should be able to replace the line and mechanism without proprietary consumables.

## Impact

A 100-net paired field pilot should compare this module with standard legal marking over a fishing season, recording lost-net incidents, actual gear recovered, unintended releases, collisions, entanglement, retrieval time and plastic fragments. At 10,000 eligible nets installed, there would be at most 10,000 backup markers; the number of animals saved or tonnes of plastic recovered cannot be inferred from that deployment count. Benefits depend on prompt fisher response and on whether releases remain discoverable in local currents. Within 10–20 years, engineering and testing of the existing timer, float and line components is feasible; adoption and ecological value remain unproven.

**Safety and lifecycle:** A premature release can create a navigation hazard and loose line can entangle wildlife. Trial only with fisheries authorities, local fishers and vessel-safety review; specify conspicuous marking consistent with local rules, closed spool routing, snag-load limits, a protected manual disarm during hauling, and a plan to recover every surfaced float. A jammed timer or fouled spool must not detach a free-floating plastic module or impair normal net retrieval. Inspect before every set; retire cracked cages or frayed line. Recover polymers and metal springs at end of life, and record lost modules. No claim of biodegradable ocean disposal is made. If the pilot shows more entanglement or litter than recovery benefit, discontinue the design.
