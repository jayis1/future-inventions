# Heat-Safe Transit Shelter

**Author: jayis1. Status: TRL 2 research concept; no measured cooling, safety certification or health benefit is claimed.**

## Problem

People waiting for a bus can be exposed to direct sun, hot seats and heat radiated from pavement even when a conventional shelter blocks overhead sun. A Guangzhou survey of 373 bus shelters and summer measurements found that shelter shade did not necessarily make waiting comfortable; a Houston field study found that enclosed shelters could make heat stress worse than an unsheltered location.[1][2] These are site-specific observations, not estimates of the number of people this proposal could protect. Riders who cannot stand for long, older people and children need an accessible waiting place; an enclosed, stagnant box can be a poor substitute for an open, well-shaded stop. Trees and safe route planning should be considered first where feasible.[2]

## Solution

A repairable, **unpowered, water-free transit shelter** for an eligible, locally surveyed stop uses a 3 m × 2 m modular canopy over an accessible waiting bay. Its upper roof is a replaceable, factory-coated light-colored aluminum sheet, specified for initial solar reflectance ≥0.75 and thermal emittance ≥0.85 **as material procurement targets**, not measured shelter performance. A ventilated 80–120 mm cavity separates it from a matte, fire-rated underside liner that blocks a direct view of the sun-heated upper sheet. Open eaves and a screened ridge let buoyant warm cavity air escape without circulating roof air into the waiting zone. Removable perforated side fins on the sunward side are oriented from an on-site morning/afternoon shade survey; they must not obstruct driver sightlines or the accessible approach. A pale, matte, replaceable seat surface is shaded at seated height. The opposite sides stay open for crossflow and visibility.

The site survey maps summer sun, pedestrian boarding paths, vehicle visibility, wind and stormwater before a local engineer sizes the bolted recycled-aluminum or galvanized-steel frame, anchors and foundation for local wind/seismic codes. Stainless, captive, corrosion-compatible fasteners use isolating washers where dissimilar metals meet. Roof runoff drains to an existing approved drainage path, not onto the boarding area. No fan, evaporative water, battery, camera, tracking or network is required. This is shade infrastructure, **not** a promise to lower ambient air temperature or prevent heat illness.

## Key Innovation

Cool roofs, ventilated double skins, shade fins and benches are prior art. The proposed contribution is a **site-indexed, replaceable radiant-shield kit**: a roof-to-underside air gap, surveyed low-sun fins and seat shading are configured together against a pedestrian-height *radiant exposure* test while preserving an open air path and wheelchair boarding clearance. The hypothesis is that a whole-stop retrofit with independently replaceable panels can outperform a same-footprint, open conventional shelter on mean radiant temperature (MRT) and seat contact temperature without worsening ventilation. The repository's [Shade-Wick Heat Vest](../shade-wick-heat-vest/) cools an individual using water; [Thermoregulatory Metamaterial Habitation](../thermoregulatory-metamaterial-habitation/) concerns a building envelope; [Repairable Roadside Noise Baffle](../roadside-noise-baffle/) targets traffic noise. None is a transit-stop radiant-shade and accessibility trial. Existing shaded stops and trees, rather than those inventions, are the real comparators.

**Unverified pilot targets**, on matched sunny summer days with air temperature 30–40 °C and identical stop orientation and pavement: ≥3 °C lower seated-height MRT and ≥5 °C lower seat-surface temperature than a same-footprint conventional single-roof open shelter, and ≥70% of the designated waiting footprint shaded at 09:00, 12:00 and 16:00 local solar time. Additionally require no worse measured pedestrian-height air speed than the comparator by more than 0.1 m/s at matched ambient wind conditions; if wind is near calm, report the ratio and absolute speeds without asserting a meaningful difference. These are experiment gates, not achieved performance. A white roof can reflect sunlight toward drivers or pedestrians; its finish and angle must pass measured glare and roadway-sightline checks, not just a reflectance specification.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 target at 1,000 kits/year: US$1,800 ex-workshop per 3 m × 2 m kit**, consisting of coated aluminum roof US$480, vented underside and spacers US$220, frame US$300, replaceable fins US$180, bench and armrests US$200, captive fasteners/seals US$120, and fabrication/quality checks US$300. These are planning allocations, not supplier quotes. **US$3,500–5,500 installed** is a separate unvalidated target assuming an existing permitted, suitable pad and US$1,700–3,700 for survey, site engineering, anchoring, labor and permits; new foundations, utility diversion, right-of-way acquisition, drainage redesign and traffic control may cost more and make the target fail. Inspect and clean twice yearly and after storms; budget an unquoted US$80–150/site-year for cleaning, inspection and minor panel replacement, excluding damage repair. A ten-year frame life and five-year coating/liner life are goals subject to corrosion, UV, fire and fastener trials. Quote full lifecycle cost against trees and maintained conventional shelters before procurement.

## Impact

At 1,000 kits/year, the production ceiling is 1,000 stops/year, **not** a population or illness-prevention estimate. First measure roof reflectance/emittance, flame behavior and corrosion resistance in a laboratory. Then compare ten paired stops for a full hot season, alternating candidate and conventional shelter designs at matched sites where practical. Log paired MRT at seated and standing heights with calibrated globe/air/wind instruments, solar irradiance, relative humidity, seat contact temperature, air speed, shade footprint at three solar times, glare at driver approach, and timed wheelchair boarding. Stratify by sun angle, humidity and wind; record cloudy days and weather differences rather than attributing every difference to the kit. Include a nearby tree-shaded stop where available. Publish failures and adverse observations, not just peak-hour gains. Changes in ridership, health outcomes and heat illness would require separate adequately powered studies; they are not inferred from MRT.

**Failure and exclusion gates:** reject configurations that worsen a standard heat-stress metric such as WBGT in humid still-air conditions, create unsafe hot seats or glare, obstruct wheelchair turning/boarding or emergency egress, block sightlines, or divert rain across the boarding path. Test low-angle side sun, wind-driven rain, clogged ridge screens/drains, fastener loosening, vandalism and flame spread; check the underside for heat accumulation and verify ventilation after soiling. An engineer must certify anchorage, sharp-edge safety, fire performance and local accessibility/roadway rules before public installation. Keep the waiting area open if a fin is damaged; isolate and repair unsafe panels. Surfaces and metals should be separable for repair and recycling; measure coating degradation and embodied impacts rather than assuming the kit is greener. A credible 10–20-year route is material qualification, paired field pilots, accessibility/fire certification and then locally fabricated variants, not a claim that passive shade replaces hydration, heat alerts or access to cooled refuge.
