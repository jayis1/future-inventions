# Lead-Line Release Profiler — Impact Analysis

**Author: jayis1.** Illustrative engineering scenarios, not a forecast, measured result, health-impact estimate or regulatory endorsement.

## Basis and boundaries

The reference method is individually numbered consecutive first-through-fifth-liter bottles, without stopping the tap, at applicable lead-service-line sites under the US EPA's 2024 LCRI protocol.[1] This proposed rack does not detect or remove lead; the additional research fractions are not evidence of a health benefit. Geography: a hypothetical US sampling program; horizon: one operating year after a successful pilot, with one five-fraction session per site. Every number below is an assumption or calculation, not an observed performance or market size.

Assume 50 / 500 / 5,000 site-visits in low / base / high scenarios; 5 L and 5 separately cleaned bottles per visit; rack reuse at most 100 sessions/year; rack purchase at the README's **unquoted 2038 US$38 target**; bottle-and-cap purchase at the README's **unquoted US$10–25 per visit target**. Assume one rack can serve 100 visits despite cleaning, transport and scheduling: this requires validation. Lab analysis for each requested fraction, shipping, worker time, QA, cleaning, preservation and failed-run repeats are excluded and could dominate. All purchases are incremental to an existing loose-bottle program, not savings.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Low | 50 | 250 L | 250 | 1 | $38 | $500–1,250 | $538–1,288 |

Calculations: liters = 5 × visits; bottles = 5 × visits; racks = ceiling(visits / 100); rack spend = $38 × racks; bottle spend = ($10 to $25) × visits. These are purchase requirements, not net costs relative to a loose-bottle baseline, which also buys bottles. At 50 sites the one-rack acquisition cost allocates to $0.76 per visit; at 500 or 5,000 sites and exactly 100 sessions per rack, $0.38 per visit. Neither figure includes maintenance or depreciation after the year.

## Potential benefit and uncertainty

The measurable benefit is *fewer unflagged order or volume errors*, not fewer lead exposures. The design gate is ≥99% correctly ordered and sealed fractions across 500 supervised five-bottle sessions; report raw failures, uncertainty and the matched loose-bottle rate. If both methods already achieve that gate, the rack may add cost and handling without benefit. A proposed sensitivity example—not an observed effect—would be 2% erroneous sessions with loose bottles versus 1% with the rack: at 500 visits that is 10 versus 5 affected sessions, or 5 potentially avoided errors. Session errors and fraction errors must not be conflated; the actual denominator and classification must be specified before a trial. Additional field time, bottle contamination and sample loss might reverse the apparent gain.

A 50% drop in assumed rack reuse from 100 to 50 sessions doubles the base case rack count from 5 to 10 and rack purchase spend from $190 to $380. Doubling annual visits doubles water and bottles, but racks increase in steps. No avoided illness, elevated-lead cases, replacement rate, emissions savings or exposure reduction is quantifiable from this concept: those require measured sampling error, verified actions prompted by results and independent exposure follow-up. A low reading never clears a site, and decisions must follow local rules and accredited laboratory results.[1]
