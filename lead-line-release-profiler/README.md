# Lead-Line Release Profiler

**Author: jayis1. Status: research concept; not an approved compliance sampler, lead detector, or declaration of safe drinking water.**

## Problem

Lead from service lines, solder and fixtures can appear as dissolved metal or intermittent particles. A single first-draw sample can miss a later volume of water that stood in a lead service line; flow changes can also dislodge particles. US EPA's 2024 tap-monitoring protocol therefore specifies paired first- and fifth-liter samples for sites with lead service lines, while research uses sequential profiles to investigate sources.[1][2] Households facing pipe replacement need reliable evidence about *where and when* release occurs, not a misleading negative from one convenient bottle. The number of households served by this device and any health benefit are unknown.

## Solution

A trained utility or community sampling worker uses a **portable, offline, five-fraction tap sampling rack** after a documented stagnation period. Five individually numbered, wide-mouth, laboratory-cleaned 1 L bottles sit on a passive index plate. A removable, water-contact-qualified polypropylene chute directs the faucet discharge into the selected bottle; a one-way spring detent advances the rack only to the next numbered bottle. A visible fill mark and catch-tray scale reveal underfill or overflow. No sample is filtered or settled in the device: particulate and dissolved lead must both reach the laboratory. The device does **not** attach a restrictive nozzle to the tap and must leave the aerator as found.

The worker opens the cold tap once, at the normal glass-filling setting, and collects the five liters continuously, switching bottles without deliberately changing the faucet setting. Changeover spills are caught in a separate tray and quantified; a spill beyond a prevalidated limit invalidates the positional interpretation. In practice this design may prove unable to change bottles without losing water or altering flow. The falsification experiment below determines whether it is better than carefully numbered loose bottles. **For US regulatory compliance, follow the current regulator's prescribed bottles and method, not this rack.**

## Key Innovation

Sequential profiling, numbered bottles, and the first-/fifth-liter pair are **existing art**.[1][2] The proposed advance to test is a **mechanical chain-of-volume witness**: a one-way keyed index plate links each numbered bottle to its collection order; fill marks expose incomplete fractions; the catch tray records changeover loss. The worker caps and seals bottles immediately after the uninterrupted five-liter collection, with an observer watching open bottles during collection. It is a procedural reliability aid, **not a new lead assay**. Unlike [Electromineral Pipe Regenerator](../electromineral-pipe-regenerator/), this device neither coats pipes nor claims to monitor water safety. Unlike [Cookware Leach Verification Station](../cookware-leach-verification-station/) and [Lead-Dust Capture Shroud](../lead-dust-capture-shroud/), it samples tap water for off-site analysis rather than testing cookware or containing paint dust. It must be compared against ordinary labeled bottles and existing commercial sequential-sampling kits before claiming any advantage. A reproducible, locally manufacturable demonstrator is plausible within 10–20 years; superiority is not established.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 US-dollar design target at 10,000 racks/year:** US$38 ex-workshop per reusable rack: molded polypropylene index/chute US$15, stainless spring and captive detent US$6, washable catch tray and volume witness US$8, assembly and inspection US$9. Five certified laboratory-cleaned 1 L bottles and caps are a separate **US$10–25 per sampling event target** (US$2–5 each); accredited analysis, laboratory-prescribed preservatives, shipping, worker time, installation, distribution and certification are excluded. The rack is cleaned between sites and designed for replaceable springs and detents. If a laboratory requires its own container geometry, use that laboratory's approved containers or abandon this design. Recycle clean polypropylene at end of life where accepted; route contaminated samples and acid-preserved residues through laboratory-approved handling, never household recycling.

## Impact

A **proposed, unvalidated 50-site pilot** would compare the rack with standard individually numbered bottles for sample-order errors, missing volume, changeover loss, leakage, field time and total cost. Its primary engineering gate is **≥99% correctly ordered and sealed fractions across 500 supervised five-bottle sessions**, with zero unflagged swaps in deliberate fault trials; this is not a health or lead-removal target. Record actual lead concentration in each fraction through accredited laboratory analysis, total recoverable lead as required by the method, and separate dissolved/particulate results only when the lab validates a paired field/lab filtration protocol. Report measured error rates with confidence intervals, including invalid sessions, rather than inferring exposure reductions. A negative profile at one flow and one time is **not proof of absence of lead**; source replacement, corrosion control, certified filtration, and public-health advice remain the interventions.

## Engineering and sampling boundaries

- **Materials and envelope (targets, not ratings):** potable-water-contact-qualified polypropylene or equivalent for any wetted chute; 304 stainless detent isolated from water; laboratory-approved HDPE or PET bottles. Accept 0.5–2.0 L/min of free-falling tap flow into an atmospheric-pressure chute (never plumbed under pressure), 5 × 1.00 L fractions with proposed ±20 mL visual fill tolerance. Tap supply pressure is outside the rack's operating envelope; the rack is not connected to or rated for the supply line. A 1 L fill at 1 L/min takes about one minute; a five-liter profile at that rate takes about five minutes excluding changeovers. These are geometrical estimates, not measured performance.
- **Protocol for a research profile, not compliance:** a trained worker documents fixture, aerator condition, filters/softeners, nearby water uses, plumbing repairs, elapsed stagnation (target ≥6 h), date/time, approximate flow and sample IDs. Do not remove/clean an aerator or deliberately pre-flush before the stagnation interval. Collect 1–5 in order at ordinary cold-tap glass-filling flow, record every spill/stop and deviations, cap all bottles immediately after the five-liter draw, and follow the receiving laboratory's preservation, temperature, holding-time and chain-of-custody instructions. Never add acid in a home without the lab's approved kit and trained handling. Follow applicable local rules; this research protocol is not a substitute for EPA LCRI collection instructions.[1][3]
- **Why not rely on first draw alone:** the fifth liter can represent service-line water in some plumbing layouts, but actual residence volume varies by fixture and pipe geometry. A profile is a sequence of volumes, not a guaranteed map to a particular pipe segment. Flow affects particulate mobilization, so note actual flow; do not compare profiles at different flow settings as if they were interchangeable.[1][2]
- **Fault behavior:** no electronics or battery are required. A blocked chute, stuck detent, loose bottle, overflowing tray, disturbed aerator, leaking cap, incorrect order, uncertain stagnation or missing lab seal means **invalid profile / resample**. A visible mark cannot detect all cross-contamination. Test splash, reagent blanks, bottle carryover, low/high flow, mid-fill stop, power-independent lighting loss and user misordering under blinded observation. Provide tactile numbered detents and large-print labels; workers should not need a phone or account.
- **Validation gate:** compare paired rack and loose-bottle profiles on a controlled, lead-free test loop seeded with inert visible particles, then on approved real-world sites with independent laboratory analysis and blanks. Randomize order on repeat visits, since one draw depletes stagnant water. Pre-register acceptable changeover loss (proposed ≤10 mL per transfer), bottle recovery, false-order and contamination rates. If the chute changes the flow/particle profile, loses more water, or offers no measurable reduction in errors versus existing kits, reject the rack rather than claiming diagnostic improvement. Obtain sampling-program and laboratory approval before any field interpretation.
- **Privacy and lifecycle:** labels need anonymized site/sample identifiers for laboratory traceability, not resident names on the rack; custody records remain with the authorized program. No camera, radio, location tracking or automated household scoring. Wash and inspect the rack between sites; replace worn springs and damaged chutes. Laboratory disposes of lead-bearing samples under local requirements. No chemical treatment or discharge is produced at the tap beyond collected spill water, whose handling must follow site guidance.
