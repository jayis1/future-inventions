# Passive Flood Door Sill

**Author: jayis1. Status: TRL 2 concept; not a certified flood-protection product.**

## Problem

Floods affected more than **2 billion people worldwide during 1998–2017**, according to WMO.[1] A shallow, slow-rising inundation can enter a building through a doorway, but simply sealing an unsuitable building can concentrate water pressure against weak walls or foundations. Floodproofing never replaces evacuation, elevation, or land-use planning.[2]

## Solution

A **removable 0.6 m-high doorway panel** for an *engineer-approved, low-velocity, above-grade masonry or reinforced-concrete building* uses a recycled-aluminium frame, replaceable EPDM perimeter gasket, and stainless-steel cam anchors into a verified structural surround. A shallow outside-facing pressure pocket uses rising water pressure to push the panel's gasket into its frame; manual cams provide initial preload before water arrives. No power, wireless connection, camera, or proprietary cartridge is required.

A separate **mechanical pressure-differential relief hatch** near the panel base opens inward when a site-specific, engineer-set pressure threshold is exceeded. It deliberately admits water if holding it out would impose an unsafe load; a visible red float flag indicates that protection has been lost. The hatch is not a substitute for engineered foundation flood openings, and the panel must not be installed where such openings are required. The builder sizes anchors, relief flow and threshold against the *whole building's* walls, floor and other water paths, not the door alone.[2]

## Key Innovation

Pressure-seated seals and flood panels are established. The proposed advance is a **repairable entry barrier with a mechanically independent, calibrated load-limiting inlet**: under modest head it keeps out water, but at a certified pressure differential it intentionally yields before the structure is asked to resist more. The research question is whether the hatch can equalize pressure quickly enough under realistic rising-water rates while retaining useful protection at lower heads; neither performance nor patent novelty is established. Unlike the repository's seismic-metamaterial-isolation-foundation (earthquake wave modification) and thermoregulatory-metamaterial-habitation (building heat transfer), this targets shallow flood entry with an explicit structural-safety release.

## Target Cost

**Unverified 2035 factory target:** US$90–140 per 0.9 m-wide panel at 100,000 units/year, including aluminium frame and panel, EPDM seal, mechanical latch, relief hatch, assembly and quality control. This excludes site survey, structural reinforcement, installation, taxes, replacement seals and any flood insurance. A target US$8–15 replaceable seal kit and annual pre-season inspection must be compared with local sandbags, certified barriers and elevation. The cost target is not a quotation and does not imply a given household can safely use the device.

## Impact

A trial at **100 eligible buildings** would test a maximum **0.6 m static water depth** and a specified slow-rise condition set by a structural engineer; the proposed bench target is **less than 1 L/min ingress per metre of sealed perimeter at 0.3 m head** after 100 wet/dry cycles. Measure leak rate, anchor displacement, differential-pressure release, relief flow and time to equalization; these are unverified design targets, not field results. At 100,000 installed panels, up to 100,000 eligible doorways might have *one* entry path protected, conditional on independent certification, maintenance and building-level assessment. No estimate of lives or homes saved is warranted.

**Limits and fail-safe:** Never use below grade, at high flow velocity, in wave/surge or debris-impact zones, on weak walls, or as a refuge strategy. A blocked hatch, failed anchor, hidden alternate water path, or damaged gasket defeats protection. Inspect before each flood season, test the hatch with clean water, replace failed seals and recycle metal parts at end of life. If the flag appears or water enters, do not try to reseal against pressure; evacuate according to local warnings. Independent structural and hydraulic testing, local floodplain approval, and community trials over a 10–20 year development horizon are prerequisites to any deployment. No military, surveillance or control application is intended.

## How It Works

1. Before a forecast flood, a trained occupant inspects the door surround and hatch, places the removable panel in the fitted threshold and closes the stainless cam anchors. The cams preload the EPDM gasket; this is **not** an automatic rising barrier and offers no protection if nobody installs it in time.
2. As water rises slowly outside, pressure in the shallow face pocket presses the panel into its seal. At 0.6 m static head, freshwater pressure at the base is about 5.9 kPa. The triangular load on a 0.9 m-wide opening is `F = ρgh²w/2 ≈ 1.59 kN`, acting 0.2 m above the base; the corresponding moment is about 0.32 kN·m. These idealized hydrostatic numbers exclude current, impacts, buoyancy under the floor and loads on the rest of the building.
3. If the *measured differential pressure* exceeds an engineer-set limit, an independent spring/diaphragm hatch opens inward to admit water and reduce differential load. The red float flag shows loss of protection. Hatch capacity must be proven against site-specific rise rates; opening is not proof that the structure is safe.
4. When water recedes, do not uncam a loaded panel. After authorities deem entry safe, inspect for contamination, drain and clean the pocket and hatch, check anchors and gasket, and replace damaged parts before reuse.

## Technical Architecture

The replaceable aluminium panel and frame carry pressure through cam anchors into surveyed jambs and a verified floor/threshold substrate; a gasket seals the perimeter, not the masonry beyond it. A mechanically separate differential-pressure hatch has an inlet screen accessible for cleaning, an adjustable *engineer-sealed* setting, a visible open-state flag and a drain/inspection port. It needs no power or data link. The physical flow is outside water → face pocket → pressure-seated gasket; above the allowed differential it becomes outside water → relief hatch → inside wettable area. A test rig must measure actual hatch flow and structural deflection before fixing the setting. Specify 316 stainless fasteners, a corrosion-resistant aluminium alloy and replaceable EPDM seals; final alloy, anchor spacing and seal geometry depend on exposure and engineered substrate tests. This complements, rather than replaces, whole-building wall sealing or backflow prevention.[2]

## Deployment Scenarios

- **Terraced home on an above-grade street:** A surveyed, reinforced doorway with a predictable slow-rise warning gets a stored, pre-fitted panel. Inspect and deploy before water arrives; pair it with drain backflow control and wall-path assessment. No deployment if the required exit would be blocked.
- **Small ground-floor shop:** Staff fit the panel before closing when a municipal flood alert arrives; the float flag makes any pressure-relief event unmistakable at reopening. Inventory still must be raised, and a separate safe egress route is required.
- **Above-grade utility annex:** Facilities staff maintain identical panels and documented pre-season tests across eligible service doors. Critical circuits remain elevated; below-grade rooms, fast-flowing water and debris-impact sites are excluded.

## Risks & Mitigations

- **Egress and accessibility:** A fitted 0.6 m panel can obstruct evacuation, while a threshold can create a trip or wheelchair barrier. Use only where a qualified local review approves an independent exit and a compliant dry-state threshold. An interior release is **not** an assured escape under flood load; evacuate before fitting if no safe exit remains.
- **Pressure and alternate ingress:** Anchors, walls or floors can fail before the hatch relieves pressure; water can also enter vents, service penetrations and drains. Survey the whole structure, test hatch flow and add appropriate backflow/wall measures; never seal a required foundation flood opening.[2]
- **Jamming or corrosion:** Silt, leaves, ice and chloride exposure can block the hatch or cams. Provide inspectable screens, clean after each exposure and wet-test before each flood season; reject use if the mechanism sticks.
- **Leakage and nuisance release:** Gasket compression set, misalignment and uneven substrates increase seepage; a mis-set hatch or cleaning water can trigger unintended ingress. Gauge the fit and calibration, replace worn seals, shield the sensing pocket from splashes and test both slow rise and hose exposure. No setting may be casually raised to stop nuisance opening.
- **Overtopping and false confidence:** Above 0.6 m, in fast flow, or if the relief flag rises, the protected-room claim ends. Keep flood warnings and evacuation plans primary; after a release, assume interior contamination rather than reclosing against pressure.

## Vision for 2050

In a plausible 2050 scenario, building-level flood surveys identify the few above-grade openings where a repairable, load-limited panel is safe; those panels are maintained alongside backflow valves and resilient interiors. Standardized independent testing and trained installers could make that narrow retrofit repeatable. Where elevation, relocation or deliberate wet floodproofing is safer, those approaches win instead.[2]
