# Post-Fire Runoff Cassette

**Author: jayis1** · TRL 2 research concept; neither removal performance nor ecological benefit has been demonstrated for this design.

## Problem

After wildfire, rain and snowmelt can mobilize ash, sediment, nutrients and metals into streams; effects vary dramatically by catchment.[1] A USGS study in Montana found increased soluble reactive phosphorus and turbidity after fire, but not a significant increase in total phosphorus, illustrating why a universal post-fire removal claim would be unsound.[2] Ash-laden pulses can impair habitat and burden downstream water treatment, while the steepest flow pulses can also erode road crossings. The intended users are watershed crews responsible for small, permitted roadside outfalls in burned catchments, not households seeking drinking water.

## Solution

Install an **off-line, removable treatment side-cell downstream of an existing culvert or road drain**, only after a hydraulic engineer verifies that the original drainage path remains fully available. A passive, adjustable side-branch skims up to **1 L/s** from the original channel into a 200 L accessible settling chamber and ten parallel, separately handled 50 L beds of washed iron-oxide-coated sand. An upstream coarse rack catches debris; a low-shear inlet and sump retain some ash and grit; ferric oxyhydroxide surfaces can bind dissolved phosphate. The side-cell returns water to the same permitted drainage route. Excess flow takes the original, visibly labeled **untreated** channel. No gate or filter is placed across the existing culvert throat. A rising water level, blocked rack or ice must divert flow to that original path, not back water onto the road.

The ten sealed beds total 500 L of media. Assuming 40% effective water-filled void volume, 200 L / 1 L/s gives **200 seconds nominal empty-space contact time**; short-circuiting, media packing and frozen flow can reduce actual contact. The saturated 50 L trays may weigh well over 50 kg; require a rated mechanical lift and trained service crew rather than manual carrying. Inspect after each significant event; remove accumulated sediment and change media on measured phosphorus breakthrough or excessive head loss, not a fixed visual color cue. Pilot sampling ports on inlet, treated outlet and bypass make whole-event load accounting possible. No additives, engineered organisms, cameras or remote telemetry are required.

## Key Innovation

Iron-enhanced sand filtration and sediment basins are established, not newly invented: field studies of agricultural tile-drain filters have measured phosphorus retention, with maintenance required to avoid clogging.[3] The research proposition is a **rapidly deployable, off-line post-fire side-cell** with independently cleanable ash sump and modular sorbent beds, designed to preserve the original road-drain hydraulic capacity while explicitly measuring untreated bypass. It tests whether temporary treatment *after* a fire is useful under fast, variable ash pulses when permanent upstream infrastructure is absent. The central uncertainty is whether short contact and ash fouling leave enough dissolved-phosphorus capture to justify installation and service. No worldwide novelty or patentability claim is made.

Unlike the [Storm-Drain Tire-Toxin Trap](../storm-drain-tire-toxin-trap/), which fits inside a curb inlet for chronic tire-chemical runoff, this is an **external, temporary side-cell at a burned-catchment outfall**, focused on ash solids and dissolved phosphorus while leaving the drain throat untouched. Unlike the [Snowmelt Brine Accounting Gate](../snowmelt-brine-accounting-gate/), it does not measure or reuse chloride. It is not a potable-water device, and iron media cannot be presumed to remove every metal or organic contaminant.

**Unvalidated pilot gates:** for site-approved 1 L/s diverted flow at 5–25 °C and defined ash/solids loading, target ≥80% mass removal of suspended solids **within treated flow** and ≥50% soluble reactive phosphorus mass retention within treated flow over a specified cumulative volume before replacement. Report all bypass volume and load separately and calculate whole-storm capture; reject any design that reduces the existing crossing's approved drainage capacity, including rack-blocked and ice-loaded cases. These are proposed tests, not outcomes or universal environmental standards. A 10–20-year pathway runs from paired bench columns and flood-safe hydraulic trials to permitted multi-season catchment pilots.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 planning target in US dollars at 1,000 modules/year:** US$5,000 ex-workshop for a galvanized-steel support and HDPE 200 L settling chamber (US$1,500), ten 50 L lined media trays and initial tested iron-coated sand (US$1,800), bypass/interface fittings (US$700), sampling ports and assembly/quality checks (US$1,000). Target US$400 for a complete replacement set of washed media, before return freight and disposal. Site survey, engineered civil tie-in, road-access safety, labor, permits, laboratory assays, storm repairs and spent-media classification/disposal are excluded; installed lifetime cost may exceed the ex-workshop estimate substantially. Compare against approved erosion control, a conventional sediment basin and no device before procurement.

## Impact

At rated flow, one continuously fed cell could route **3.6 m³/hour** through treatment, but rainfall is episodic and the untreated bypass may dominate the highest pollutant loads. A planning fleet of 1,000 units has at most 1,000 L/s simultaneous **nominal routed capacity**, not a measured reduction in catchment phosphorus, stream toxicity or treatment cost. Record incoming and both outgoing flow-weighted solids and dissolved-phosphorus loads, then report bypass-inclusive removal with uncertainty. The desired benefit is less post-fire pollutant mass reaching vulnerable streams and reservoirs, contingent on paired field evidence; source control and watershed restoration remain primary.

**Failure and validation controls:**

| Failure or misuse | Detection and safe state | Recovery and unresolved risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ash, leaves or ice plug side-cell | Water-level inspection and a witnessed blocked-rack trial; original full-capacity drainage path remains open | Crew clears sump/rack; bypassed polluted water is counted, not described as treated |
| Media saturates, channels or releases iron/phosphorus | Paired lab samples for soluble reactive P, Fe, Mn, pH and suspended solids; head-loss check | Isolate and replace trays; single-point samples may miss a first-flush breakthrough |
| Extreme flood, undermined footing or road hazard | Engineer-approved head–flow and erosion tests including complete side-cell obstruction | Remove unit if road safety or bank stability cannot be maintained; independent original path must still pass design storm |
| Contaminated spent media or worker exposure | Characterize ash and used media including leachability under local rules | Seal, label and send to authorized handling; never assume land application or ordinary disposal |

Compare at least ten representative burned and unburned runoff matrices in bench tests, including high alkalinity and high organic carbon; then pair treated and control outfalls across storms and seasons. A null or negative whole-storm load change, pollutant leaching, frequent unsafe blockage, or no advantage against maintained conventional controls falsifies the central proposition. Obtain site and discharge approvals before field use. This concept makes **no potable-water, fish-survival or clinical safety claim**.

## How It Works

A site engineer first checks the culvert's design-storm rating and approves a downstream side-branch that cannot constrict the original outlet. A coarse, accessible rack and adjustable gravity takeoff admit at most 1 L/s into the 200 L HDPE chamber. Slower flow gives coarse ash and grit a place to settle; the sump is emptied between events before solids can wash back out. The remaining water splits among ten parallel 50 L iron-oxide-coated-sand trays, then rejoins the permitted drain. Iron surfaces offer a finite number of phosphate-binding sites; dissolved metals, organics and pathogens are **not** certified treatment targets. The main channel carries every liter not diverted, including blocked-side-cell flow, as untreated runoff.

The 500 L media inventory at an assumed 40% water-filled void fraction gives 200 L nominal water inventory and 200 s nominal residence at 1 L/s, **not** a guaranteed contact-time distribution. At 0.1 L/s per tray the nominal inventory is 20 L per tray. A field crew records inlet, treated outlet and bypass flow plus time-matched samples across a storm; outlet/inlet soluble reactive phosphorus (SRP) breakthrough and measured head loss trigger replacement. If access is unsafe, ice forms or a rack blocks, stop treatment and leave the original route unobstructed. Seal sampled spent material, classify it under local waste rules, and lift loaded trays mechanically.

## Technical Architecture

| Subsystem | Water, signal or service path |
| --- | --- |
| Existing culvert and independent bypass | Pass the site's approved peak flow even when the entire side-cell is obstructed; inspect scour and backwater before permitting. |
| Takeoff, rack and settling chamber | Limit and distribute diversion; debris stays at a removable rack and settleable solids in an accessible sump. |
| Ten parallel media trays and return manifold | Isolate a tray for service without claiming that bypassed water is treated; return treated water downstream to the same authorized route. |
| Pilot metering and sampling ports | Pair inlet/outlet/bypass volume and flow-weighted TSS, SRP and relevant site-specific analytes; log head loss and field maintenance. Passive unit needs no telemetry. |
| Waste and worker interface | Contain wet ash and heavy spent trays, use a rated lift, lab-test leachability and document authorized disposal. |

No actuator or sensor is allowed to close the original culvert. On a failed sampler, missing bypass record or unverified flow split, the event's removal estimate is invalid rather than silently extrapolated.

## Performance Benchmarks

This design has **no measured prototype results**. A field study of an agricultural tile-drain iron-enhanced-sand filter reported 64% ± 8% phosphate mass capture (95% confidence interval, 31 monitored events), with maintenance to preserve flow.[3] That study used sand mixed with iron filings, **not** this proposed iron-oxide-coated sand, and its hydrograph, ash loading and bypass differ: its percentage is context, not a transferrable post-fire performance guarantee. A maintained conventional settling basin plus no-device monitoring should be field comparators; their removal rates must be measured at the same site rather than assigned an invented state-of-the-art percentage.

| Metric (test boundary) | Proposed gate, not achieved | Comparable baseline or needed evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diverted flow and residence (clean water, 5–25 °C) | ≤1 L/s; 200 s nominal void-volume residence at full rate; tracer-test distribution | Measure conventional basin residence at identical flow; nominal time is not effective contact time. |
| Hydraulic safety (design storm, blocked rack and frozen side-cell) | Zero loss of approved original crossing capacity | Engineer's pre-installation head–flow curve versus installed, fully blocked configuration. |
| Treated-flow TSS mass (defined ash matrix and particle-size distribution) | ≥80% over a specified cumulative test volume | Parallel basin/blank measurements, influent concentration and particle-size-resolved outlet data. |
| Treated-flow SRP mass (specified influent concentration, pH, alkalinity and flow) | ≥50% until measured breakthrough; define that cumulative volume in bench tests | Parallel iron-sand and unsorbed-sand columns, plus inlet/outlet load curves; no transferable published percent assumed. |
| Whole-event load, head loss, media life and service | Report bypass-inclusive removal, pressure/head rise, m³ to breakthrough, crew-hours and installed US$/m³ | Measure against conventional controls across multiple storms, including missed-service cases; no numeric claim yet. |

The targets above apply only to the **diverted fraction**. If, hypothetically, 75% of the incoming pollutant load enters the side-cell and treated-flow TSS removal meets 80%, whole-event removal is 60%, not 80%; an actual site must measure load split, not infer it from the water-volume split.

## Deployment Scenarios

1. **Burn-scar roadside outfall:** a road authority installs a permitted unit downstream of a small culvert with independent peak-flow capacity; a trained crew checks the rack after each major rain and removes sump solids without obstructing traffic.
2. **Reservoir feeder channel:** a utility pilots paired treated and control outfalls upstream of a vulnerable intake, sampling SRP and ash during the first wet season. Operators keep drinking-water treatment unchanged; the cassette is not a potable-water barrier.
3. **Temporary municipal drainage retrofit:** a municipality tests modular cells at accessible post-fire stormwater outfalls, with documented bypass, scheduled storm-response crews and a contracted waste route. Sites lacking safe lifting access or a demonstrably free bypass are excluded.

## Risks & Mitigations

- **Flash-flood blockage and scour:** never put media in the culvert throat; verify the fully blocked side-cell against the original design storm and remove the installation if backwater or erosion rises. Untreated bypass remains a real environmental loss.
- **Ash fines, ice and short-circuiting:** bench-test worst-case solids pulses and frozen configurations, use removable racks and tracer tests, and suspend diversion when service cannot be assured. Fine particles can still escape.
- **Sorbent breakthrough or leaching:** sample flow-weighted SRP, Fe, Mn and pH at both outlets and replace media on a tested breakthrough/head-loss limit. Site chemistry may make iron media ineffective or release bound material.
- **Waste, lifting and uncertain benefit:** use rated lifts and lab-based waste classification; include road closure, lab work and disposal in lifecycle cost. Even a successful treated-flow trial may deliver no measurable watershed-scale benefit against restoration or an ordinary basin.

## Vision for 2050

If multi-season trials demonstrate safer hydraulics and lower whole-event pollutant loads at an acceptable installed lifecycle cost, watershed agencies could stock standardized side-cells for rapid, temporary deployment after fires. Shared monitoring protocols would let crews prioritize the few outfalls where treatment demonstrably beats source control and a maintained basin; units would return to a depot for cleaning and materials recovery after the burn scar stabilizes. Where the evidence fails, the responsible 2050 outcome is **not** ubiquitous hardware but better restoration and runoff management.
