# Slope-Head Sentinel — Impact Analysis

**Author: jayis1.** Scenario projections, not observed lives saved or certified protection. Scope: site-assessed shallow slopes where positive head at the selected screen is relevant. The proposal does not address rockfall, deep failures, unsaturated suction or remote-source debris flows. [README](README.md) gives mechanism and sources.

## Baseline and accounting boundary

WHO reports more than 18,000 landslide deaths and 4.8 million people affected globally in 1998–2017 (README source [1]); those figures are **not** a denominator for this device. The fraction attributable to eligible, instrumented shallow positive-head sites is unknown. A rainfall-only official warning, professional assessment and drainage/stabilization remain the comparison baseline; incremental warning benefit cannot be assumed. Count a site only once, even when it has multiple instruments. No published pilot data establish sensitivity, false-alarm rate, lead time, or reductions in harm.

## Transparent 10-year deployment scenarios (illustrative)

Assume one device and one assessed site per row unit, 40 residents **near** each site, US$65/device ex-factory target at 10,000/year, and US$150–400/site one-time investigation/installation/service planning allowance. The 40-resident assumption is deliberately distinct from the proposed pilot's 10,000/40 = 250 residents per slope; both are occupancy scenarios, not measured exposure. Costs exclude tax, distribution, multi-borehole investigations, recurring inspections, training, replacement and evacuation logistics. The $65 target may not hold at smaller volumes.

Pilot planning is **40 sites / ~10,000 residents**, not 40 × 40. Pilot hardware at the aspirational $65 price would be $2,600, and modeled one-time total $8,600–18,600; actual pilot fabrication and geotechnical work may cost substantially more. At the conditional scale, hardware alone is $650,000 and the services allowance $1.5m–4m. None of the residents-near-sites counts proves that a person observes a flag or can leave safely.

## Conditional warning-value model

For any chosen decade, let *E* be people actually exposed to an eligible event at instrumented sites, *D* the incremental probability that a usable flag appears with enough lead time beyond the baseline warning, *O* the probability it is observed, and *R* the probability of a safe response given observation. Then *E × D × O × R* estimates **people with an opportunity for an additional effective response**, not deaths avoided. As an arithmetic sensitivity example only: if E = 1,000 distinct exposed people, D = 0.2–0.6, O = 0.5–0.9 and R = 0.3–0.8, the modeled opportunity is **30–432 people per decade**. All input ranges are hypothetical, not measured forecast skill or compliance. Avoided injuries, deaths, displacement and loss cannot be estimated responsibly without site event rates, counterfactual official-warning performance and outcome data.

False alerts also have costs: if 1,000 sites each issue an average of 0.1–1 unnecessary red flags/year, that is 100–1,000 investigations/year before any closure or evacuation cost, assuming independent sites and complete observation. This is a sensitivity range, not a measured alarm rate; repeated alarms may reduce O and R. Include staff time, lost access and alarm fatigue in prospective evaluations, and do not suppress a justified red flag simply to improve the metric.

## Evidence gate and externalities

The pilot must document each reference-head trace, watch/red/invalid flag, official warning, known slope event and failure without flag, plus actual observer and response actions. Independently assess false and missed events by slope type before claiming benefit. A passing monthly mechanical check is **not** event-detection sensitivity. Future evaluations should compare matched sites or historical baselines with explicit uncertainty and weather severity, not attribute all harm reduction to the device. Manufacturing and disposal of HDPE, polypropylene and stainless steel, drilling disturbance, travel for maintenance and accessible communication costs belong in a lifecycle assessment. Retire or retrieve failed units; prioritize drainage or engineered stabilization when they provide stronger risk reduction.
