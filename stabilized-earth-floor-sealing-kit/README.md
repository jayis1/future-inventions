# Stabilized Earth Floor Sealing Kit

**Author: jayis1**

**Category:** Healthy Housing & Universal Basic Services

**Status:** Concept (TRL 2; not a qualified building product)

## Problem

Many millions of people live with bare-earth floors that shed dust, admit mud, and are difficult to clean. Replacing a floor with a poured concrete slab can demand paid transport, cement, equipment, and skilled labor. A coating that traps moisture or erodes within a rainy season is not a solution. This concept is for sound, non-flooding ground-floor rooms; it cannot fix a wet foundation or prevent disease on its own.

## Solution

A locally mixed, compacted 20 mm earthen topping uses graded soil/sand, hydrated lime, and calcined clay (a reactive pozzolan). Lime supplies calcium hydroxide; the clay reacts with it in moisture to form calcium-silicate/aluminate hydrates that bind soil grains. A reusable hand roller and thickness rails set compaction and coverage. An inexpensive absorbent paper moisture witness under a clear patch checks whether the existing floor stays damp before installation. If it does, stop and address drainage and a suitable damp-proof assembly rather than sealing it in.

The homeowner or trained local mason screens the soil, adds measured water, rolls the topping in thin lifts, protects it from drying too fast for at least seven days, then applies a thin, repairable limewash. No battery, sensor, personal data, or proprietary binder is required. The material can be mixed with local aggregate after bench testing for grading and contaminants; respirators and gloves are needed when handling dry lime and clay.

## Key Innovation

Lime-stabilized earth and pozzolanic floors already exist. The proposed advance is a repairable, low-cement floor *kit* that makes moisture rejection, aggregate grading, measured compaction, controlled curing, and simple patch repair part of the product rather than leaving these critical steps to guesswork. It is distinct from this repository's carbon-capture building materials (bulk mineralized construction products) and seismic foundation (earthquake isolation): its purpose is affordable, washable interior floor surfacing, not structural or carbon-credit performance.

## Target Cost

**Design target, not a supplier quote:** US$5–8/m² in materials at 10,000 kits/year in 2035: approximately US$2–3 for lime and calcined clay, US$1–2 for screened aggregate and pigment/limewash, and US$2–3 for packaging, waste allowance, and shared roller amortization. For a 12 m² room: US$60–96 materials, excluding labor, substrate repairs, drainage, transport, taxes, and finance. A ten-year service target with US$1/m² of patching over that period implies US$0.60–0.90/m²-year before those exclusions. Local quotes and durability trials could disprove this target.

## Impact

A pilot of 100 rooms would compare dust loading, wet-cleaning time, floor moisture, abrasion, and repair frequency against untreated earth floors and locally available cement floors over two wet seasons. A scale scenario of 100,000 rooms of 12 m² each would cover 1.2 million m², not necessarily 100,000 newly healthy households. Health outcomes must be independently measured; no disease reduction is claimed. Training and locally sourced mineral inputs could support community-owned installation and repair without surveillance, military, or coercive applications.

## Feasibility and limits

The underlying lime–pozzolan reaction and hand compaction are established; consistent performance with variable local soils is not. Reject floors with rising damp, flooding, contaminated soil, or structural movement until those causes are addressed. The finish is not a load-bearing slab or a substitute for a damp-proof membrane. See [SPECIFICATION.md](./SPECIFICATION.md) for the testable mix, mass balance, failure gates, and development path.

## How It Works

First, inspect the room for seasonal flooding, soft subsoil and rising damp. An overnight sealed-patch moisture witness is a rejection screen, not a substitute for an assessment across seasons. Screen the candidate soil for organics and contaminants; sieve and trial compact it with 0–4 mm washed sand. Mix by *dry mass* with 7.5% hydrated lime and 7.5% calcined kaolinitic clay, then trial approximately 8–12% water by dry-mix mass. These are preliminary laboratory proportions, **not a universal household recipe**. Local clay reactivity, shrinkage and exposure tests determine whether the mix is usable.

Over a sound compacted base, rails and a reusable hand roller establish a 20 mm compacted topping. At the target dry density of 1,600 kg/m³, that is 32 kg of dry material per m²; the substrate must bear this added load. Moist curing for at least seven days supports the lime–pozzolan reaction: dissolved silica and alumina react with calcium hydroxide to form mineral hydrates around the grains. A thin, replaceable limewash reduces surface dust and accepts localized repair without an impermeable oil film. Verify dryness and surface hardness before normal wet cleaning; cold or humid rooms need longer. This is a wear surface, not waterproofing or structural reinforcement.

Why not concrete or an existing improved-earth floor? Concrete is often the more appropriate choice where a qualified structural slab is needed. EarthEnable already installs compacted earthen floors with a polymerizing drying-oil seal and reports an approximately US$4/m² selling price in its operating region; that is **its reported product**, not a quote for this kit.[1] Here the proposed distinction is a mineral limewash finish and explicit soil/moisture rejection and repair gates in a reusable kit—not a claim to have invented earthen flooring. The US$5–8/m² kit **materials target excludes installation** and cannot be compared directly with an installed selling price.

## Technical Architecture

The system is a sequence of material and decision gates, not electronics: site inspection → moisture witness and soil screening → local trial mix and compaction → protected cure → limewash → inspection and patch repair. The physical modules are measuring buckets and sieve, premeasured lime/calcined-clay supplies, local graded aggregate, thickness rails, hand roller and tamping block, and a field instruction/record card. No app, sensor or resident data is required.

Per m² the preliminary mass budget is 27.2 kg mineral fraction, 2.4 kg hydrated lime, 2.4 kg calcined clay and 2.6–3.8 kg initial water. A 12 m² room needs 384 kg dry mix; carrying, ventilation, door clearance and floor bearing matter. Record soil source, moisture observation, mix batch, compacted thickness, cure conditions and any cracks on the card. A damp witness, suspect source soil, debonding or connected cracks over 1 mm triggers stop/rework rather than a cosmetic topcoat. The finish is vapor-tolerant, **not proof against capillary water**. See the proposed tests in [SPECIFICATION.md](./SPECIFICATION.md).

## Performance Benchmarks

No kit performance has been measured. The comparison below deliberately separates a bare-earth control and a *different* installed oil-sealed earth product from this mineral-topping concept; none is an interchangeable structural assembly.

| Metric / test condition | This kit: unverified design target | Current comparator / evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| Material price, USD/m², 2035 at 10,000 kits/year | $5–8, excluding labor, drainage, transport and testing | EarthEnable **reports** ~$4/m² for its installed oil-sealed floor in East Africa; geography, finish and inclusions differ.[1] |
| Dry topping mass, kg/m² at 20 mm | 32 at 1,600 kg/m³ dry density | No matched comparator measured; structural concrete slabs are not equal-thickness substitutes. |
| Protected cure before acceptance | ≥7 days, longer if cold/humid; verify hardness/moisture | EarthEnable **reports** 2–3 weeks total installation-to-use for its different assembly.[1] |
| Wet abrasion/dust, matched local soil and traffic | Less mass loss and dust than paired bare earth over two wet seasons; numeric threshold pending baseline | Bare earth and local cement-floor controls to be measured in the same pilot. |
| Cracks / repair over one year | Reject connected cracks >1 mm; failure if patching >10% area/year | No comparable local baseline yet. |
| Embodied carbon, kg CO₂e/m², cradle-to-gate | Not yet quantified; regional lime/clay energy and transport LCA required | GSA publishes concrete EPD limits in kg CO₂e/m³ by strength class,[3] but converting them into an avoided floor impact needs a functionally equivalent local slab design. |

The ten-year service period is a **hypothesis**, not a warranty. A paired study must also test wet slip resistance, surface water uptake, vapor transport, indoor particulate exposure and cleaning-agent compatibility before any hygiene or emissions advantage is asserted.

## Deployment Scenarios

- **Rural room retrofit:** A household and trained local mason screen a dry, stable 12 m² room and trial the local soil; the US$60–96 material target is only a starting procurement budget. Compare total installed cost and maintenance with available improved-earth and concrete offers, and preserve access to the room during cure.
- **Temporary shelter upgrade:** A humanitarian buyer could deploy reusable rollers across sound, non-flooding rooms, but only after local acceptance tests, adequate cure space and a repair plan. Do not install on waterlogged ground or promise rapid occupancy; a separate drainage/flood solution is necessary.
- **School or clinic ancillary room:** A facilities buyer would prioritize cleanability, slip testing and documented batch quality. This is not yet a qualified clinical floor or infection-control surface; use an approved floor wherever regulation or wet-cleaning duty demands one.

## Risks & Mitigations

- **Variable or contaminated soil:** expansive clay can shrink and crack, and contaminated soil can expose residents. Screen sources and trial at least three soils; reject failed mixes and hazardous feed rather than adjusting the recipe by eye.
- **Damp and movement:** limewash can wear or lose adhesion; a vapor-open finish does not solve capillary rise, hydrostatic pressure, seasonal flooding or unstable subsoil. Stop, remediate the site separately and reassess; never use the kit as a damp-proof membrane.
- **Installer exposure and quality:** hydrated lime is caustic and dry handling creates respirable dust. Use eye/skin protection, dust controls, wet mixing where practicable, trained supervision and a recorded compaction/cure check; test indoor particles after abrasion.
- **Wear, cleaning and falls:** furniture legs and repeated wet cleaning can abrade limewash; wet floors may be slippery. Measure wet slip and abrasion, schedule inspection and patching, and retire a failing installation if annual patching exceeds 10% of area.
- **Supply and evidence gap:** calcined clay is energy-intensive and may not be locally reactive; lime and transport prices vary. Verify binder reactivity, regional lifecycle emissions and full installed cost against local alternatives. No controlled health outcome or field durability result exists for this kit. Mexico's Piso Firme study concerns **cement floors**, not this composition, and cannot establish disease reduction here.[2]

## Vision for 2050

If the trials clear moisture, indoor-air, durability and affordability gates, a local mason could offer a documented repairable floor upgrade where a full slab is unnecessary. Training cooperatives could share rollers, source tested mineral inputs and maintain floors rather than sell a one-time mystery coating. By 2050 the useful outcome would be an affordable, inspectable floor option accepted by local housing guidance—not a claim that every dirt floor can safely be sealed. Flooded, contaminated and structurally deficient rooms still require different interventions.
