# Staged Enamel Repair Strip

**Author: jayis1. Status: TRL 2 concept; not a validated dental treatment.**

## Problem

WHO estimates that oral diseases affect nearly **3.7 billion people**, and identifies untreated decay in permanent teeth as the most common health condition in the Global Burden of Disease 2021.[1] This figure covers *all oral diseases*, not a population eligible for this strip. Early, non-cavitated enamel lesions may be reversible, but access to preventive dentistry is uneven; once a cavity forms, a surface strip cannot restore missing tooth structure. Sugar reduction, fluoride exposure and toothbrushing remain the foundation of prevention.[1]

## Solution

The **Staged Enamel Repair Strip** is a disposable, tooth-sized, saliva-wetted two-stage film for a dental worker to apply to a *confirmed non-cavitated enamel lesion*. A thin cellulose-backed, hydroxypropyl-methylcellulose contact layer first releases a self-assembling peptide of the P11-4 class into a cleaned lesion. A peel-away barrier then exposes a second layer containing separately dry-stored calcium and phosphate salts in food-contact polymer microdomains. Saliva hydrates this layer and supplies ions to the peptide-treated surface. Calcium-phosphate precipitation and peptide templating are established chemical ideas; **this sequence, formulation and clinical outcome are not demonstrated here**. The removable backing is recovered as clinical waste, not swallowed or left in the mouth overnight.

A worker screens for cavitation, pain, swelling, and other warning signs before use. Those findings require conventional dental assessment rather than another strip. The product would supplement, not replace, fluoride toothpaste, diet changes or professional care.

## Key Innovation

P11-4-class peptides have already been investigated for remineralizing early lesions; this is **not** a claim to have invented the peptide or enamel repair.[2][3] The proposed difference is a low-cost, *physically staged* single-site delivery format: peptide enters the lesion before a separately stored ion source is exposed, reducing premature calcium-phosphate precipitation in the package and allowing a controlled comparison with peptide alone and ordinary fluoride care. A visible peel tab marks the second stage without electronics, a phone, patient tracking or a proprietary reader. Whether peptide can penetrate sufficiently under real saliva and plaque conditions, and whether the staging improves clinical outcomes, are the central unanswered questions.

**Unverified research targets:** one strip covers a 10–20 mm tooth-surface span; the peptide contact stage lasts 5–10 minutes and the ion stage another 10–15 minutes under supervised placement. A target shelf life of 18 months at 30 °C in a moisture-barrier pouch is conditional on stability data. Bench assays must quantify peptide release, depth of uptake, salt release and unwanted precipitation under variable salivary flow and pH; a randomized clinical study would then compare lesion progression at 6 and 12 months against standard fluoride prevention and peptide-only care. No cavity-reversal percentage or clinical benefit is claimed.

## Target Cost

**Planning target, not a supplier quote:** US$0.30–0.60 per sealed strip at 1 million units/year in 2035, including two films, pouch, assembly and rejects; the peptide and barrier-packaging yield are the principal uncertain cost drivers. A two-application course would consume US$0.60–1.20 of strips, *excluding* dental examination, worker time, transport, taxes, disposal and follow-up. Procurement should compare the total cost per lesion that remains non-cavitated against inexpensive fluoride varnish and toothpaste, not compare strip price alone. Publish the formulation and permit qualified regional manufacture if safety and performance are established.

## Impact

At a hypothetical **1 million strips/year** and two strips per assessed lesion, maximum nominal capacity is **500,000 lesion courses/year**; this is arithmetic capacity, not 500,000 cavities prevented. Count examined lesions, eligible patients, completed applications, adverse events and independently assessed non-progression at 12 months. Wider benefit depends on the incremental efficacy over existing fluoride-based care and on affordable screening; neither is known. A successful 10–20-year development programme could offer a shelf-stable, instrument-light adjunct where dental visits are scarce, but would not eliminate the need for clinicians or address advanced decay.

## Limits and safety gates

- Fail closed on a breached pouch, visible film contamination, ambiguous lesion classification or inability to remove the backing. Never apply to a cavitated tooth or present pain/infection as a strip-treatable problem.
- Test mucosal irritation, peptide sensitization, aspiration/choking risk, accidental ingestion, polymer residues and bacterial contamination before human trials. Children and pregnant patients require separate eligibility and safety evidence.
- High saliva flow may wash peptide out; acidic conditions may dissolve newly deposited mineral. Require controlled in-vitro cycling, tooth-slice microscopy and blinded clinical endpoints before efficacy claims.
- The chemistry is not an antimicrobial cure; unnecessary treatment can delay a filling or mask disease. The strip collects no data and has no surveillance or coercive use.
- End-of-life is a small mixed-film medical-waste stream; quantify pouch and backing mass and pursue recyclable packaging only where infection controls allow.
