# Tidal Marsh Sediment Ratchet

**Author: jayis1. Status: untested restoration concept, not a flood-defense structure.**

A hand-serviceable, one-way-*resistance* baffle for sheltered, sediment-bearing tidal creeks; it does not make water flow one way.

## Problem

Salt marshes protect coastal habitat and communities, but rising relative sea level can outpace their *net surface elevation gain*. Sediment deposited on the surface can compact or subside, so a thick visible layer is not proof that marsh elevation is keeping pace.[1] Local restoration often uses coir logs to slow waves and encourage deposition, but these degrade, can lose height, and are unsuitable for energetic shores.[2] A village-scale intervention must also preserve tidal exchange, fish passage, drainage, and neighboring shorelines. This is a coastal-ecosystem problem across many regions, not a claim that one device can restore every marsh.

## Solution

A coastal cooperative or restoration agency installs short, removable **1 m modules** inside a surveyed, low-energy tidal creek margin or abandoned drainage cut—not across its full channel. Each module has an open-bottom timber or recycled-HDPE frame, uncoated stainless pivot pins, replaceable woven coir slats and an adjustable stop. Incoming tide lifts compliant slats into a more open configuration; ebb flow presses them toward a perforated stop, increasing drag in the *local* marsh-side pocket while maintaining a permanently open aquatic bypass. The lower ebb velocity may let fine mineral particles settle in vegetated cells rather than exit on the next tide. No pump, battery, network, chemical, or imported sediment is required. The slats are not check valves; both flow directions remain possible.

A local crew can remove a clogged slat with hand tools without disturbing the frame. Installation requires local hydrodynamic and habitat approval; where fish movement, flood conveyance, navigation, or shoreline access cannot be maintained, the design is ineligible. The coir is inspected after storms and replaced only after reviewing abrasion, wildlife entanglement and sediment quality. The structure is temporary; remove it when vegetation stabilizes or if it worsens erosion.

## Key Innovation

**Falsifiable design hypothesis:** a hinged, *asymmetric-drag* coir baffle with a permanently open relief path retains more sediment in an adjacent planted marsh cell than a fixed coir log of equal material mass **without** increasing flood stage, scour, wildlife obstruction, or down-current erosion. Coir logs and sediment-trapping reefs already exist; a three-year field experiment with biodegradable reefs found localized accretion but also localized scour, underscoring why net effects cannot be inferred from deposition behind one barrier.[2][3] The proposed advance is the removable directional-drag geometry and whole-cell sediment/flow accounting, not invention of coir, tidal restoration, or a proven sediment pump. Closest repository concepts, **Peatland Carbon-Lock Rewetting Array**, **Ocean Alkalinity Restoration Array**, and **Alternating Recharge Terrace**, respectively manage freshwater peat, seawater chemistry, and inland aquifer recharge; none tests tidal-creek ebb/flood drag asymmetry for salt-marsh elevation.

## Target Cost

**Unquoted 2038 manufacturing goal at 10,000 modules/year:** US$57 ex-factory per 1 m module: coir slats US$18, locally cut frame US$12, corrosion-resistant pins and stops US$11, reusable anchors US$9, assembly US$7. Target 3-year installed lifetime with annual inspection and one US$18 slat replacement (US$75 hardware over three years, before installation). Site survey, permits, sediment assays, storm repairs, wages, disposal and ecological monitoring are excluded and may dominate cost. Compare a *site-specific installed bid* with fixed coir logs and a no-structure restoration control; there is no verified price or performance advantage. No proprietary consumables; eligible local fabrication should avoid treated wood and loose plastic fragments.

## Impact

A **proposed pilot target**, not a measured result: at 20 paired sheltered marsh cells over two full years, achieve at least **2 mm/year greater net surface-elevation gain** than equal-mass fixed coir-log controls, with no statistically supported adverse change in adjacent-cell elevation, tidal inundation, flood stage, or fish passage. A surface-elevation table plus marker horizons distinguishes elevation gain from deposition and shallow subsidence.[1] An illustrative 10,000 sites × 20 m² suitable treatment cell covers **200,000 m²** of candidate habitat, not 200,000 m² restored; deployment must follow independent ecological trials. Benefits could include habitat persistence and coastal buffering, but avoided flood damage, carbon credits and biodiversity gains require direct measurement. Sediment-starved sites or marshes with blocked landward migration may not benefit even if the device works locally.[4]

## Validation, limits and stewardship

- Map baseline flow, salinity, turbidity, wildlife routes, vegetation, upstream water levels and sediment contaminants; obtain permits and community/Indigenous consent. Use a fixed-coir and an untreated control at comparable elevations. Bench-test cycle fatigue, 0.2–1.0 m/s alternating flow, slat loss and debris jams; these are **test conditions**, not certified operating ratings. Reject any design that blocks emergency drainage when every moving slat jams.
- Record stage and velocity on both sides during spring/neap tides and storms; measure net sediment budgets, adjacent scour and marsh elevation quarterly for at least 24 months. Predefine stop gates: a sustained rise in upstream high-water mark beyond locally approved tolerance, loss of aquatic passage, entanglement, contaminated deposition, or measurable harm to neighboring marsh means remove the modules.
- A hinged flap can pin shut, detach as litter, trap fish, or shift erosion onto neighbors. Pin retention, large unobstructed openings, hand-removable coir and after-storm inspection reduce—but do not eliminate—those risks. Never call this a storm barrier or install it where it substitutes for engineered flood protection. No surveillance or personal data is collected.
- Existing coir logs, timber framing and flow-measurement methods are mature; the 10–20-year work is hydrodynamic qualification, ecological safeguards, locally repairable manufacturing and comparative field replication, not novel physics.
