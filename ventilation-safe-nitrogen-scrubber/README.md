# Ventilation-Safe Nitrogen Scrubber

**Author: jayis1. Status: research concept; performance, cost and field safety are unvalidated targets.**

## Problem

Ammonia (NH₃) lost from animal housing is both a nitrogen resource loss and a precursor to fine particulate pollution and damaging nitrogen deposition. Agricultural air emissions affect farm workers and neighboring communities; the US EPA monitors ammonia and other pollutants at animal feeding operations.[1] The potential beneficiaries therefore include millions of people exposed to agricultural air pollution, but this device treats only the fraction exhausted through an eligible, mechanically ventilated barn—not open yards, manure lagoons or land application. Owners are farms or cooperatives with an existing forced-ventilation exhaust, a trained service provider and a lawful fertilizer outlet.

## Solution

A bolt-on, washable exhaust cassette places a **low-obstruction, acid-recirculating spray stage** in a parallel duct beside a full-size, gravity-opening bypass. Polypropylene mist eliminators and a polypropylene/FRP wetted shell contain an aqueous sulfuric-acid loop; ammonia dissolves and reacts as `2 NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄`. An externally bunded HDPE tank holds the solution for laboratory testing, lawful agricultural reuse or approved off-site disposal. A coarse washable dust screen precedes the sprays, and a removable demister prevents acidic droplets escaping the outlet. No animal-contacting liquid or open acid reservoir is allowed.

A spring/gravity bypass opens mechanically when duct differential pressure rises above a barn-specific commissioned limit, or if the module is isolated; the existing ventilation fan and its independent alarm remain in control. Loss of power or pump flow stops acid dosing and opens the bypass, sacrificing capture rather than animal ventilation. This is an exhaust treatment, **not** a substitute for fresh-air requirements or manure-source control. Farmers inspect the bypass indicator daily; qualified technicians handle acid, calibrate sensors, remove captured dust and test the recovered liquid. No camera, animal identification, cloud service or person-level data are required.

## Key Innovation

**Acid spray scrubbers and ammonia-to-ammonium-sulfate recovery are existing art.** A full-scale poultry study reported 71–81% NH₃ removal at <25 Pa pressure drop under comparatively concentrated 66–278 ppmv exhaust.[3] Gas-permeable ePTFE membranes have also been piloted in livestock housing, but measured area-normalized recovery was only 2.3 g N/m²/day in one pig-house pilot and 0.4 g N/m²/day in a poultry-house pilot; dilution by summer ventilation limited uptake.[2] The proposed advance is a standardized *ventilation-priority retrofit*: serviceable spray/demister cassettes coupled to a **power-independent, pressure-actuated full-flow bypass**, independent fan alarm and measured nitrogen/acid inventory. It must prove that this safety architecture and nutrient recovery work economically at much lower inlet concentrations; neither the scrubber chemistry nor the claimed performance is new or demonstrated for this design.

**Falsifiable design target, not measured performance:** on a representative forced-ventilation exhaust averaging **20 ppmv NH₃ at 25 °C**, treat **10,000 m³/h** and remove **≥70% of inlet NH₃** over a 30-day service cycle while adding **≤40 Pa** clean-to-service pressure loss. An independent engineer must first certify the site's allowable pressure rise, ventilation capacity and safe bypass flow. Lower inlet concentration may render the recovery uneconomic; the system must not incentivize reducing ventilation to increase yield.

A transparent steady-state balance at those test conditions uses **24.45 L/mol** ideal-gas molar volume: inlet is about **0.115 kg nitrogen/h**; 70% capture is **0.080 kg N/h**, consuming a theoretical minimum **0.281 kg pure H₂SO₄/h** and yielding **0.378 kg dry ammonium sulfate/h** (about **1.92 kg N/day**, **6.73 kg acid/day**, **9.07 kg dry salt/day** at continuous full load). Acid make-up, dissolved water, dust and other absorbed gases increase handled liquid; these numbers are stoichiometric ceilings for fertilizer yield at the assumed inlet, not predicted field harvests. At 40 Pa, 10,000 m³/h and 60% incremental fan efficiency, extra fan demand is about **0.185 kW**, or **2.3 kWh/kg captured N** before recirculation-pump power; measure the *full* energy budget. A spent or bypassed cassette captures nothing.

## Target Cost

**Unvalidated 2038 target at 10,000 units/year: US$12,000 ex-factory per 10,000 m³/h module**: FRP/PP duct and bypass US$3,000, PP spray/demister and screen US$2,200, pump and corrosion-resistant piping US$1,800, HDPE bund/tank and valves US$1,500, pressure/pH/flow instruments plus independent alarm interface US$1,500, assembly/leak test US$2,000. Allow **US$4,000** for survey, ductwork, commissioning and ventilation certification; exclude taxes, finance, site electrical upgrades, transport, acid, labor, laboratory certification and fertilizer distribution. Over a ten-year nominal life the installed hardware alone is **US$1,600/year**, before replacement and financing. At the modeled 20 ppmv/70% point, theoretical annual acid use alone is roughly **2.46 tonnes of pure H₂SO₄ equivalent** if operated continuously, so fertilizer value cannot be assumed to pay for the plant. Budget and compare local acid supply, water, pump/fan electricity, technician visits, licensed liquid disposal and existing proven scrubbers before purchase. Cooperative ownership and pooled acid delivery may help smaller farms, but a site without safe chemical handling is not eligible.

## Impact

A first **10-site, 12-month paired retrofit trial** compares treated and bypassed exhaust under identical mandated ventilation, reporting hourly mass flux, inlet concentration, seasonal removal, pressure drop, power, acid consumption, fertilizer contaminant tests, bypass time and maintenance cost. Theoretical 10,000 modules at the stated continuous-load point would capture up to **about 7,020 tonnes N/year**; actual capture must be calculated from measured duty cycle and mass balance, not installed count. The initial decision gate is ≥70% seasonal removal at qualifying concentrations, ≤40 Pa pressure rise throughout the cleaning cycle, no reduction in minimum ventilation, no uncontained acid release, and a site-specific positive net environmental and economic case relative to a conventional scrubber. Stop or redesign if a bypass jams, fan capacity is compromised, outlet mist exceeds permitted limits, fertilizer fails contaminant standards, or acid and energy burdens outweigh nitrogen recovery. This system cannot by itself prevent ammonia emissions from fields or lagoons, nor establish a health benefit without exposure studies.

## Safety and validation

Use two independent pressure measurements at commissioning, a physical bypass-position flag, secondary containment, corrosion inspection, and periodic independent fan-flow verification. Dust clogging or demister scaling raises pressure and must trigger bypass and a local alarm; pump or mains failure stops capture but leaves ventilation unobstructed. Sensor drift must not disable the mechanical relief path. A ruptured acid line requires pump shutdown, spill containment, trained response and off-site remediation; never divert acidic product directly to crops or waterways. Sample the product for pH, nitrogen, pathogens, metals and applicable fertilizer limits before use; reconcile purchased sulfur, captured N and disposed residuals. Replace PP/FRP and HDPE components via established recycling streams where possible; manage spent liquid and contaminated screens as regulated waste.

Within 10–20 years, bench-test bypass opening with a stopped fan and dust-loaded cassette, conduct an independently witnessed ventilation-safety pilot, then trial seasonal farm operation and third-party fertilizer compliance. The nearest repository concepts recover nitrogen from **drainage nitrate** (`electrogenic-denitrification-drainage-array`) or synthesize fixed nitrogen from air (`nitrogen-fixing-bioreactor`); neither captures **volatilized barn NH₃ from exhaust**. This is a proposed integration of known chemistry and ventilation hardware, not a patent-novelty claim.
