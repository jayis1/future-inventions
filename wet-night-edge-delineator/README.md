# Wet-Night Edge Delineator

**Author: jayis1** · Research concept; no safety or performance claim has been field-validated.

## Problem

The WHO estimates **1.19 million road-traffic deaths per year**, with more than half among pedestrians, cyclists and motorcyclists.[1] That global figure is context, **not** the subset this device could prevent. On unlit rural roads, a person walking or cycling near the edge and a driver both need a legible road boundary, especially during rain. Conventional paint can lose much of its nighttime visibility when a water film changes the optics: FHWA reports that dry retroreflectivity cannot predict wet performance.[2] Existing raised reflective markers help but can be damaged by plows, traffic and dirt; paint, lighting, speed management and protected paths remain essential.

## Solution

A road authority or local road-maintenance cooperative installs a **replaceable, two-sided optical edge marker** on an approved, non-travelled shoulder, after a site-specific traffic-safety review. The proposed 120 × 65 × 35 mm body is recycled aluminum with rounded edges and a bolted, replaceable clear polycarbonate insert. The molded polycarbonate microprism facets sit under a shallow, downward-draining canopy so rainwater runs past rather than forming a continuous film over the optical face. A white face points toward approaching traffic; an amber reverse face can be fitted only where the local road-marking code permits it. Stainless fasteners anchor a deliberately frangible mounting foot to a surveyed, buried base outside the travelled path. No LEDs, battery, sensor, camera, radio or power source are needed: headlamp rays return toward the driver through total internal reflection in the microprisms.

Installers position the marker according to the authority's sight-line and spacing standard, never as an improvised lane marking or a substitute for a pedestrian barrier. A maintenance worker wipes the face during scheduled inspections, swaps a scratched insert without removing the base, and removes a loose, broken or displaced unit immediately. Its visible maintenance witness is a **separate recessed reference patch** protected from abrasion; a large contrast difference between the exposed face and the patch prompts cleaning, but is not a calibrated substitute for photometric inspection. The mount must break away under a certified impact load without leaving a sharp obstruction. Recycle metal and segregate damaged polymer inserts; do not wash road grime into a drain.

## Key Innovation

Raised pavement markers, microprism retroreflectors and sheltered roadside delineators already exist. The research contribution to test is a **serviceable wet-optic insert plus an independently sacrificial roadside mount and visual fouling comparator** in one low-cost edge unit. The canopy trades exposed optical area for water shedding; its geometry may reduce off-axis return, while dirt and condensation can negate the claimed wet advantage. There is no claim of patent novelty or a demonstrated crash reduction.

**Unverified qualification targets:** at a specified headlamp/observer geometry chosen by the road authority, retain **at least 50% of the new dry luminous-intensity return under continuous artificial rain** after 12 months of equivalent UV, abrasion, splash and thermal cycling; maintain detectability at **50 m** from a representative low-beam vehicle at **40 km/h** in a closed-course user study including older drivers; and replace an insert in **under 5 minutes** with basic hand tools. These are product-specific acceptance targets, not the FHWA pavement-marking thresholds in mcd/m²/lx; test and report actual marker luminous-intensity coefficient in cd/lx at stated angles and the rain rate. A successful reflector cannot make a pedestrian visible, guarantee stopping distance, or justify higher speeds.

## Target Cost

**Unverified 2038 manufacturing target at 100,000 units/year:** US$6 ex-factory per unit (US$1.80 recycled-aluminum shell/foot, US$1.40 molded optical insert, US$0.80 fasteners/seal, US$2 assembly, inspection and packaging). Replacement insert: US$2. Assume, only for a planning scenario, **200 markers/km** (5 m spacing) and a **five-year** average service life: US$1,200/km upfront and US$240/km-year of hardware amortization, before installation, bases, road closure, labor, cleaning, lost units, transport, permits, financing and replacement inserts. The road authority must price those items and compare actual installed lifetime cost with compliant raised markers, delineator posts and wet-reflective markings; at many sites the incumbent will be cheaper or safer. No cost-per-life-saved estimate is defensible before a crash study.

## Impact

At the assumed 200/km, **100,000 installed units would delineate at most 500 km** of eligible road. That is coverage, not people protected or crashes avoided. First run a 20-km paired pilot against the road authority's best compliant incumbent: measure wet and dry angular photometry after each rainy season, nighttime detection distance, glare, displacement, debris, cleaning hours, insert losses and traffic conflicts. Independent road-safety researchers should assess speed compensation and crash/near-miss outcomes over longer matched corridors, not extrapolate benefit from brightness alone. Reject the design if wet visibility is inferior to the incumbent, attachments create a collision hazard, maintenance is unaffordable, or markers encourage unsafe speed.

The hardest unsolved issue is retaining a useful headlamp-return angle while shedding water and grime without dangerous roadside protrusions. Bench-optics proof of concept is approximately **TRL 2** for this integrated design; gated laboratory, closed-course and authority-permitted field testing over **10–20 years** would be required before broad use. Breakaway mounts can still become debris; vandalism, standing water, snowplows, vegetation, fog and non-motorized users outside the beam remain residual hazards. The device has no sensing or person-level data collection and is not intended for military, surveillance or control applications. It complements, rather than replaces, slower speeds, protected walking/cycling routes and maintained road markings.
